The 5-Phase Sequence
0
Assess — Read Your Map
Before anything else · 1–2 weeks
›

You cannot treat what you cannot see. The biggest mistake in gut healing is starting a protocol before knowing which organisms or dysfunction is actually present. Get the data first.

Priority Tests

FIRST CHOICE
Organic Acids Test (OAT) — maps yeast, fungal, and bacterial overgrowth via metabolic byproducts. Arabinose (Candida), Tartaric (Aspergillus), HPHPA/4-Cresol (Clostridia). Great Plains / Mosaic Labs.
ADD IF ABLE
GI-MAP (Diagnostic Solutions) — PCR-based stool test. H. pylori, pathogens, parasites, fungi, zonulin, SIgA, calprotectin, beta-glucuronidase.
IF SIBO SUSPECTED
SIBO Breath Test (hydrogen + methane, 3-hour lactulose) — determines bacterial type and guides antibiotic selection.
LEAKY GUT
Cyrex Array 2 — intestinal permeability panel. Or use zonulin from GI-MAP as proxy.
If you can't get labs yet: Start with the symptom-based path in the Decision Tree tab. A terrain-preparation protocol (diet + binders + digestive support) is appropriate for anyone regardless of lab status.
1
Prepare — Open Drainage & Clear Terrain
Weeks 1–4 · Binders · Diet · Drainage support
›

Before clearing organisms, the drainage system must be functional. If you start antifungals or antimicrobials into a congested system, die-off toxins recirculate — causing the herxheimer reactions that make people quit protocols.

Drainage First

BILE FLOW
TUDCA 250–500mg with meals. Supports bile acid production, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, liver protection, gut motility. Critical when mold or mycotoxins are part of the picture.
BINDERS
GI Detox (clay + charcoal + zeolite), activated charcoal, or cholestyramine (Rx). Intercepts mycotoxins and endotoxins in the gut before they recirculate. Take 30–60 min before meals OR at bedtime. Always separate from medications and supplements by 2+ hours.
BOWEL REG.
Magnesium bisglycinate 300–500mg PM. If constipated, toxins stagnate. Must have 1+ formed bowel movements per day before aggressive clearance.
DIET
Remove: sugar, alcohol, processed foods, high-mold foods (peanuts, corn, dried fruit, aged cheese). Add: clean protein, non-starchy vegetables, healthy fats. See Nutrition tab for condition-specific adjustments.
Sensitive patients: Run Phase 1 for a full 2–4 weeks before adding clearance agents. Opening drainage before clearance determines whether the protocol is tolerable.
2
Clear — Targeted Antimicrobial / Antifungal
Weeks 4–12 · Organism-specific · Overlaps with Phase 3
›

The organism determines the protocol. See the Decision Tree and Medications tabs for specific agents. Clearance is never one-size-fits-all — matching the antifungal or antimicrobial to the confirmed finding is the difference between results and relapse.

By Condition

CANDIDA
Nystatin (non-systemic) OR Fluconazole OR Itraconazole. Herbal: caprylic acid, berberine, oregano oil. Duration: 4–8 weeks minimum.
ASPERGILLUS
Itraconazole (pharmaceutical-grade — herbals insufficient). 200mg 1–2x/day. Pulsed for sensitive patients. Duration: 8–12 weeks. Continue binders throughout.
CLOSTRIDIA
Vancomycin oral (C. difficile), Metronidazole, or targeted antimicrobials. Saccharomyces boulardii concurrent. Support dopamine pathway with B6 (P5P), zinc.
SIBO
Rifaximin (hydrogen SIBO) OR Rifaximin + Neomycin (methane SIBO). Or herbal: oregano oil + berberine + allicin. 14-day course. May require 2 rounds.
H. PYLORI
Triple or Quadruple Therapy (physician-directed). Concurrent: mastic gum, DGL, bismuth, broccoli sprout sulforaphane. Retest 4 weeks post-treatment.
PARASITES
Nitazoxanide (broad antiparasitic) or Metronidazole (Giardia). Herbal: wormwood, black walnut, clove triad. 30-day protocol. Continue binders.
3
Replace — Restore What Was Depleted
Begin week 2–4 · Concurrent with Phase 2 · Continue through Phase 4
›

Gut overgrowth depletes bile acids, digestive enzymes, and key nutrients. Without restoring these, clearance doesn't hold and reseeding fails. Replacement begins concurrently with Phase 1 and continues through Phase 4.

Foundation Replacements

BILE ACIDS
TUDCA (continued from Phase 1). Phosphatidylcholine if bile composition is disrupted. Critical for fat absorption and liver protection during Rx antifungal therapy.
ENZYMES
Broad-spectrum digestive enzymes (protease, lipase, amylase) with each meal. Overgrowth suppresses endogenous enzyme production — supplemental enzymes break the fermentation-overgrowth cycle.
GUT LINING
L-Glutamine (5–10g/day on empty stomach) + Zinc carnosine (75mg/day) + Collagen/bone broth. Repairs enterocyte integrity disrupted by overgrowth and leaky gut.
NUTRIENTS
Vitamin D3 (5,000 IU), Magnesium bisglycinate (300–400mg PM), Zinc picolinate (30–50mg), B12/methylcobalamin, NAC/liposomal glutathione (mold cases). These depletions are near-universal in gut dysbiosis.
4
Reseed — Rebuild the Microbiome
Begin after Phase 2 clearance · Continue 3–6 months
›

Reseeding into an unprepared terrain is the most common gut protocol mistake. Start probiotics after initial clearance — not simultaneously. The terrain must be inhospitable to pathogens before you plant beneficial organisms.

Reseeding Strategy

SPORE-BASED
MegaSporeBiotic (Bacillus species, 2 caps/day) — survives stomach acid, promotes microbiome diversity, reduces leaky gut markers. Best-tolerated by sensitive patients. Start here.
S. BOULARDII
500mg 2x/day. Anti-Candida yeast. Competes directly with Candida for adhesion sites. Well-tolerated even during antifungal therapy. Critical if Arabinose (Candida) remains elevated.
MULTI-STRAIN
After 4–6 weeks of spore-based: add multi-strain probiotic (Lactobacillus + Bifidobacterium, 25–50 billion CFU). Strain specificity matters more than CFU count. Rotate brands every 2 months.
BUTYRATE
Sodium or calcium/magnesium butyrate 600mg–1.2g/day with meals. Primary fuel for colonocytes, supports intestinal barrier, reduces gut inflammation. Accelerates lining repair concurrent with L-Glutamine.
5
Maintain — Hold the Terrain
Month 3 onward · Retest at 6 months
›

The gut is a terrain to tend, not a problem to fix once. Recurrence is overwhelmingly a terrain failure — not a treatment failure. Mold re-exposure, sugar, stress, and poor sleep will restore dysbiosis faster than any protocol can clear it.

DIET
Maintain low-sugar, whole-food foundation long-term. Not a restriction protocol — a terrain protocol. The goal is diversity and stability.
MOLD CHECK
If Aspergillus was dominant: identify and address the environmental mold source. No gut protocol can permanently clear mold overgrowth if the exposure continues.
STRESS
HPA axis dysregulation directly disrupts gut motility, intestinal permeability, and microbiome diversity. Stress management is not optional — it is a gut intervention.
RETEST
Repeat OAT at 3–6 months. GI-MAP at 6–12 months. Results inform whether to continue maintenance or re-engage clearance. Arabinose >29 or persistent symptoms = incomplete resolution.
What success looks like: OAT markers within range. GI-MAP pathogens absent. Digestion regular with minimal symptoms. Energy improved. Brain fog reduced. No binders needed. Probiotics as maintenance only.
How to use this: Find the scenario that best matches your lab pattern or symptom picture. Multiple scenarios can overlap — apply all that apply, prioritize the dominant pattern first.
OAT-Based Paths
Scenario 1 — Candida Overgrowth
Arabinose elevated (ref ≤29) · Citramalic elevated · Sugar cravings · Brain fog · Bloating
Arabinose is a Candida metabolite. Levels above 29 indicate active Candida overgrowth. Above 60 = significant. Above 100 = severe.
  • Phase 1: Low-sugar, low-yeast diet (no fruit, no alcohol, no vinegar initially) + binders
  • Phase 2 mild–moderate: Caprylic acid 1,800mg/day + Berberine 500mg 3x/day + Saccharomyces boulardii
  • Phase 2 moderate–severe: Nystatin 500,000–1,000,000 IU 3x/day OR Fluconazole 100–200mg/day (physician-directed)
  • Phase 3: Digestive enzymes, L-Glutamine, Zinc carnosine, Vitamin D
  • Phase 4: MegaSporeBiotic + S. boulardii (continue even during antifungal therapy)
  • Retest OAT at 3 months — Arabinose should drop below 50, target below 29
Scenario 2 — Aspergillus / Mold in Gut
Tartaric elevated · 5-OH-methylfuroic elevated · Furan-2,5-dicarboxylic elevated · History of water damage / mold exposure
Multiple Aspergillus markers elevated simultaneously = significant mold burden in gut. Herbals are insufficient. Pharmaceutical antifungal required.
  • Identify and address environmental mold source FIRST — no protocol can permanently clear gut Aspergillus if ongoing exposure continues
  • Phase 1: Binders are critical here — GI Detox or cholestyramine. Drain first, clear second
  • Phase 2: Itraconazole 200mg 1–2x/day (physician-directed) — low-dose pulsed for sensitive patients
  • Add: TUDCA 500mg/day (liver protection during Itraconazole), NAC 600–1,200mg/day (glutathione support), liposomal glutathione
  • Continue binders throughout clearance — time away from all other supplements/Rx
  • Phase 4: Begin MegaSporeBiotic only after Aspergillus markers are trending down
  • Retest OAT at 3 months — all Aspergillus markers should normalize
Scenario 3 — Clostridia Dysbiosis
HPHPA elevated · 4-Cresol elevated · 4-Hydroxyphenylacetic elevated · Mood symptoms · Low motivation · ADHD-like presentation
Clostridia produce dopamine-disrupting metabolites. This is a gut problem presenting as a mood or cognitive problem.
  • Phase 2: Metronidazole (Flagyl) or Vancomycin oral for C. difficile (physician-directed)
  • Concurrent Saccharomyces boulardii (prevents C. diff recurrence, reduces Clostridia)
  • Support dopamine pathway: B6 as P5P 50mg/day, Zinc picolinate 30–50mg/day, L-Tyrosine 500–1,000mg AM
  • Berberine 500mg 3x/day (herbal antimicrobial, also anti-Clostridia)
  • After clearance: SCFA support via butyrate supplementation + fiber diversity
Symptom-Based Paths
Scenario 4 — SIBO (Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth)
Upper abdominal bloating within 1–2 hours of eating · Belching · Early fullness · Brain fog post-meals · SIBO breath test: hydrogen or methane elevated
Hydrogen-dominant SIBO = faster motility type. Methane-dominant (IMO) = slower, constipation-predominant. Different organisms require different treatment.
  • Hydrogen SIBO: Rifaximin 550mg 3x/day x 14 days (physician-directed)
  • Methane SIBO (IMO): Rifaximin 550mg + Neomycin 500mg 3x/day x 14 days (physician-directed)
  • Herbal alternative: Oregano oil 200mg + Berberine 500mg + Allicin 450mg — 4 weeks, 3x/day
  • Elemental diet (if severe or refractory): 2–3 weeks; eliminates substrate for bacterial overgrowth
  • Address root cause: low stomach acid (Betaine HCl), poor motility (prokinetics: ginger, 5-HTP, LDN), structural issues
  • After clearance: introduce probiotics slowly (spore-based first); retest breath test at 4 weeks post-treatment
  • Note: Do NOT use Lactobacillus-based probiotics during active SIBO treatment — can worsen hydrogen levels
Scenario 5 — H. pylori
Epigastric pain · Nausea · Bloating after meals · Burping · Confirmed on GI-MAP or urea breath test
H. pylori is present in ~50% of the global population. Not all cases are symptomatic. Treat when symptomatic or when associated with gastric pathology.
  • First-line Rx: Triple Therapy — PPI + Amoxicillin 1g + Clarithromycin 500mg, twice daily x 14 days (physician-directed)
  • If Clarithromycin resistance suspected: Quadruple Therapy — Bismuth + Tetracycline + Metronidazole + PPI x 10–14 days
  • Adjunct (enhances eradication): Mastic Gum 1g 2x/day, DGL Licorice 380mg before meals, Zinc Carnosine 75mg 2x/day
  • Broccoli sprout extract (sulforaphane) 50–100mg/day — clinically shown to suppress H. pylori
  • Concurrent S. boulardii — reduces antibiotic-associated diarrhea, improves eradication rates
  • Post-treatment: retest with stool antigen or urea breath test at 4+ weeks post-antibiotic
Scenario 6 — Leaky Gut / Intestinal Permeability
Food sensitivities multiplying · Systemic inflammation · Autoimmune conditions · Fatigue · Skin issues · Zonulin elevated on GI-MAP · Cyrex Array 2 positive
Leaky gut is not a single diagnosis — it is a spectrum of intestinal permeability. It rarely occurs in isolation. Address the upstream driver (overgrowth, diet, stress) and the lining simultaneously.
  • Remove: gluten, dairy, alcohol, NSAIDs, processed food — during active healing phase (minimum 8 weeks)
  • L-Glutamine 5–10g/day on empty stomach — primary fuel for enterocytes
  • Zinc Carnosine 75mg 2x/day — gut lining integrity, anti-inflammatory
  • Collagen/bone broth daily — glycine, proline, hydroxyproline for lining repair
  • Butyrate 600mg–1.2g/day — fuel for colonocytes, tight junction support
  • Vitamin D3 5,000 IU — immune regulation, tight junction protein expression
  • Omega-3 fish oil 2–4g/day EPA+DHA — reduces intestinal inflammation
  • Address upstream driver: if dysbiosis found, treat it (see other scenarios)
Scenario 7 — Parasites
Travel history · Persistent diarrhea · Alternating bowel habits · Weight loss · Identified on GI-MAP (Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Blastocystis, etc.)
Parasites are underdiagnosed in the U.S. — most standard stool O&P tests miss them. GI-MAP PCR is more sensitive. Treat confirmed findings.
  • Giardia / Blastocystis: Metronidazole 250–750mg 3x/day x 7–10 days (physician-directed)
  • Broad parasitic coverage: Nitazoxanide 500mg 2x/day x 3 days (physician-directed)
  • Herbal protocol (adjunct or milder presentations): Wormwood 500mg + Black Walnut 500mg + Clove 500mg — 30 days on, 2 weeks off, 30 days on
  • Continue binders throughout — parasites release toxins on die-off
  • After clearance: full reseeding protocol (Phase 4)
Scenario 8 — General Dysbiosis / No Clear Diagnosis
GI symptoms present · No specific pathogen identified · Low SIgA · Low microbiome diversity · Fatigue · Unclear picture
This is often a terrain problem rather than a specific pathogen. Build the foundation before targeting specific organisms.
  • Begin terrain protocol: Phase 1 (binders + drainage) + Phase 3 (replacement) simultaneously
  • Low-diversity intervention: prebiotic fiber diversity — rotate sources (inulin, resistant starch, psyllium, FOS)
  • Berberine 500mg 2x/day — broad antimicrobial, microbiome modulator, AMPK activation
  • Triphala 1g PM — bowel regularity, microbiome diversity, antioxidant
  • MegaSporeBiotic (spore-based probiotic) — builds diversity without overloading with single strains
  • Address upstream: sleep, stress, HPA axis — these are microbiome determinants, not just lifestyle factors
Three levels of gut testing: (1) Standard labs — ordered by any physician, covered by insurance, great starting context. (2) Functional stool tests — GI-MAP (PCR/DNA), GI Effects (culture + metabolites). (3) Organic Acids Test — urine metabolomics showing what organisms are doing to the body systemically. Use all three for the complete picture.
OAT — Organic Acids Test (Mosaic Diagnostics / MosaicDX)

Urine test. Measures metabolic byproducts (exhaust) of organisms — not the organisms directly. Covers 7 clinical categories across ~70 markers. Key clinical categories below.

OAT · Category 1 — Yeast & Fungal Markers
Arabinose
≤ 29 mmol/mol
Primary Candida marker. 4× upper limit = significant overgrowth. Correlates with sugar cravings, brain fog, vaginal/oral thrush. Cross-reference with stool Candida on GI-MAP or GI Effects for confirmed burden.
Citramalic Acid
≤ 3.6 mmol/mol
General yeast overgrowth marker. Less specific than Arabinose but useful in the overall pattern — especially when Arabinose is borderline.
3-Oxoglutaric Acid
≤ 0.33 mmol/mol
Yeast/dysbiosis marker. Elevated with Candida and broader microbial stress. Also correlates with mitochondrial dysfunction — dual signal.
Tartaric Acid
≤ 4.5 mmol/mol
Aspergillus (mold) marker. Nearly 5× upper limit in active mold-gut colonization. Herbals are insufficient — pharmaceutical antifungal (Itraconazole) required when this is elevated with other Aspergillus markers.
5-OH-Methylfuroic Acid
≤ 14 mmol/mol
Aspergillus marker. Elevated simultaneously with Tartaric = confirmed mold burden. Distinguish from dietary exposure (remove dietary sources for 48 hrs before test).
Furan-2,5-dicarboxylic
≤ 16 mmol/mol
Aspergillus marker. Part of the mold triad. Multiple simultaneous Aspergillus markers elevated = significant colonization requiring pharmaceutical intervention.
Furancarbonylglycine
≤ 1.9 mmol/mol
Aspergillus marker. Used in combination with the other Aspergillus markers to confirm and quantify mold gut burden.
Tricarballylic Acid
≤ 0.44 mmol/mol
Fusarium mold marker. Less common than Aspergillus but relevant in mold-heavy environments. Inhibits aconitase, an enzyme in the Krebs cycle — causes energy production disruption.
OAT · Category 2 — Clostridia Bacterial Markers
HPHPA
≤ 208 mmol/mol
Clostridia sporogenes + C. botulinum. Inhibits dopamine-beta-hydroxylase — the enzyme that converts dopamine to norepinephrine. Elevated = dopamine excess or conversion failure. Presents as mood dysregulation, low motivation, ADHD-like symptoms, OCD patterns.
4-Cresol
≤ 75 mmol/mol
C. difficile marker. Directly inhibits dopamine synthesis. Strong autism research association. Significant even at moderate elevation. Paired with HPHPA — both elevated = significant Clostridia load.
4-Hydroxyphenylacetic
≤ 19 mmol/mol
C. difficile, C. stricklandii, C. lituseburense. Dopamine pathway disruption. Even mildly elevated warrants attention when cognitive or mood symptoms are part of the picture.
3-Indoleacetic Acid
≤ 11 mmol/mol
C. stricklandii + commensal bacteria marker. Low levels may indicate microbiome depletion. High = Clostridia overgrowth. Tryptophan metabolite — connects to serotonin pathway.
DHPPA
≤ 320 mmol/mol
3-(3-Hydroxyphenyl)-3-hydroxypropionic acid. Clostridia marker. Less commonly elevated but useful in complex presentations. Elevated in some dysbiosis patterns alongside HPHPA.
OAT · Category 3 — Mitochondrial & Krebs Cycle Markers

These markers reflect energy production efficiency. Elevated = bottleneck in the TCA cycle. Gut overgrowth directly depletes CoQ10, B vitamins, and mitochondrial substrates.

Succinic Acid
≤ 2.4 mmol/mol
TCA cycle intermediate. Elevation = block at succinate dehydrogenase step. Linked to fatigue, low energy output. CoQ10 and riboflavin (B2) support this enzyme.
Fumaric Acid
≤ 0.23 mmol/mol
TCA cycle intermediate. Elevated in mitochondrial dysfunction, mold toxin exposure (fumaric acid produced by Aspergillus and Fusarium species). Correlates with muscle fatigue.
Malic Acid
≤ 1.4 mmol/mol
TCA cycle — conversion to oxaloacetate. Elevated = block at malate dehydrogenase. Magnesium-dependent step. Low magnesium = impaired Krebs cycle flow.
2-Oxoglutaric (Alpha-KG)
≤ 0.30 mmol/mol
Alpha-ketoglutaric acid. Critical TCA intermediate and ammonia detoxification molecule. Elevated can indicate ammonia load from gut dysbiosis or blocked enzyme steps (B3, B1 dependent).
Aconitic Acid
≤ 20 mmol/mol
TCA cycle. When elevated alongside Fusarium markers (Tricarballylic), suggests aconitase inhibition by mold toxins. Also elevated in heavy metal toxicity — aluminum, fluoride inhibit this enzyme.
Pyroglutamic Acid
≤ 1.9 mmol/mol
Elevated = glutathione depletion or acetaminophen (Tylenol) overuse. The body recycles glutathione through this pathway. High levels flag antioxidant system under stress — critical in mold cases.
OAT · Category 4 — Neurotransmitter Metabolites

These markers reveal what gut organisms are doing to brain chemistry. Clostridia suppress dopamine. Dysbiosis depletes serotonin. The OAT is the only standard functional test that shows this.

HVA (Homovanillic Acid)
2.3–10.8 mmol/mol
Dopamine metabolite. Low HVA = insufficient dopamine signaling (low motivation, fatigue, ADHD). Elevation with 4-Cresol/HPHPA = dopamine conversion failure. Supports need for B6 (P5P), tyrosine, and Clostridia treatment.
VMA (Vanilmandelic Acid)
1.2–7.7 mmol/mol
Norepinephrine/epinephrine metabolite. Low = epinephrine insufficiency (fatigue, poor stress response). High = adrenal hyperactivation or catecholamine-secreting tumor (rare — but worth noting when severely elevated).
5-HIAA
2.7–12.9 mmol/mol
Serotonin metabolite. 90% of serotonin is made in the gut. Low 5-HIAA = gut dysbiosis depleting tryptophan → serotonin pathway. Addresses the gut root of anxiety, sleep disruption, low mood — before trying SSRIs.
Quinolinic Acid
≤ 55 mmol/mol
Neuroinflammatory marker. Elevated = tryptophan being shunted to the kynurenine pathway instead of serotonin. Associated with depression, neuroinflammation, gut-brain axis disruption. Elevated with gut dysbiosis, mold, and chronic infections.
Kynurenic Acid
3–28 mmol/mol
Tryptophan metabolite. Inverse balance with quinolinic acid. High kynurenic = NMDA receptor antagonism (schizophrenia research). Low kynurenic + high quinolinic = neuroinflammatory pattern. B6 (P5P) and gut healing shift this balance.
OAT · Category 5 — Oxalate Metabolites

Oxalates come from three sources: diet (spinach, almonds, beets), endogenous production, and microbial production — especially Aspergillus and Candida. Mold gut burden = elevated oxalates.

Oxalic Acid
≤ 23 mmol/mol
Primary oxalate marker. Elevated in fungal/yeast overgrowth, vitamin B6 deficiency, or high dietary intake. Deposits in kidney (stones), joints (pain), and mitochondria (energy disruption). Low-oxalate diet + B6 (P5P) + calcium citrate (binds dietary oxalates).
Glyceric Acid
≤ 50 mmol/mol
Elevated = primary hyperoxaluria type II, or intermediate in oxalate metabolism. Useful differentiator when oxalic acid is elevated — helps identify the metabolic origin.
Glycolic Acid
≤ 31 mmol/mol
Intermediate in the glyoxylate-to-oxalate pathway. Elevated in primary hyperoxaluria type I, or when vitamin B6 is insufficient (B6 prevents glyoxylate from converting to oxalate).
OAT · Category 6 — Fatty Acid Oxidation Markers

These markers reflect whether the body can efficiently burn fat for energy. Elevation indicates impaired beta-oxidation — often from B2 (riboflavin) deficiency, CoQ10 depletion, or carnitine insufficiency.

Adipic Acid
≤ 0.40 mmol/mol
Impaired beta-oxidation of medium-chain fats. Elevated with riboflavin (B2) deficiency or carnitine insufficiency. Fatigue despite adequate sleep — body can't extract energy from fats.
Suberic Acid
≤ 0.52 mmol/mol
Impaired fatty acid oxidation. Elevated with CoQ10 deficiency or B2 deficiency. Mitochondrial target: CoQ10 200–400mg + Riboflavin 100mg are first-line interventions.
Ethylmalonic Acid
≤ 0.37 mmol/mol
Short-chain fatty acid oxidation marker. B2 deficiency indicator. Elevated alongside Adipic and Suberic = confirmed impaired fatty acid oxidation requiring riboflavin + CoQ10 support.
OAT · Category 7 — Nutritional & Detox Markers
Methylmalonic Acid
≤ 0.37 mmol/mol
Best functional B12 marker. Methylmalonic acid accumulates when B12 is insufficient — even when serum B12 appears "normal." Elevated = true functional B12 deficiency. Treat with methylcobalamin (not cyanocobalamin).
Pyridoxic Acid
See report range
Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) status marker. Pyridoxic acid is the urinary excretion form — low = B6 deficiency. B6 (as P5P) is critical for dopamine synthesis, oxalate control, and serotonin production.
Orotic Acid
≤ 0.30 mmol/mol
Elevated = ammonia detoxification stress or B-vitamin deficiency (B12, folate, or biotin). Gut bacterial overgrowth produces excess ammonia — orotic acid elevation reflects the downstream burden on urea cycle.
2-Hydroxyisobutyric Acid
≤ 0.22 mmol/mol
Toxic exposure marker. Elevated with MTBE (methyl tert-butyl ether) exposure from gasoline fumes, contaminated water, or fuel additives. Indicator of environmental toxic load alongside gut burden.
GI-MAP (Diagnostic Solutions Laboratory) — PCR / DNA Stool Test

Stool test. Uses quantitative PCR to detect and quantify actual organism DNA — not metabolites. Tells you WHO is there, how much, and (for H. pylori) which antibiotics won't work. 5-page report.

GI-MAP — Pathogens & Infection
H. pylori + Virulence Factors
Not detected
PCR identifies H. pylori AND its virulence genes (babA, cagA, vacA, virB, virD). CagA+ = highest ulcer/cancer risk. GI-MAP also tests antibiotic resistance genes: Clarithromycin and Amoxicillin resistance — critical before choosing triple therapy. Standard culture misses this.
C. diff Toxin A + B
Not detected
Quantitative toxin detection. Toxin A and B together = active C. diff infection. Requires vancomycin or fidaxomicin (physician-directed). Concurrent S. boulardii essential to prevent recurrence.
Candida spp.
Not detected
Stool Candida DNA (genome equivalents/gram). Cross-reference with OAT Arabinose — both elevated = confirmed significant burden. GI-MAP distinguishes Candida albicans from other species.
Giardia · Cryptosporidium · Blastocystis
Not detected
PCR detects these parasites with far greater sensitivity than standard O&P microscopy. Giardia = water-source. Cryptosporidium = immunocompromising. Blastocystis = controversial pathogen — treat if symptomatic.
Campylobacter · Salmonella · E. coli variants
Not detected
Bacterial pathogens. E. coli panel includes EPEC, EAEC, ETEC, STEC — each with distinct pathogenicity. Important after travel, foodborne illness, or persistent diarrhea.
GI-MAP — Microbiome Health Markers
Akkermansia muciniphila
Detected (adequate)
Keystone species. Lines and repairs the gut mucosal layer. Low Akkermansia = leaky gut, metabolic syndrome risk, poor immune regulation. Supported by polyphenols, pomegranate extract, and intermittent fasting.
Faecalibacterium prausnitzii
Detected (adequate)
Primary butyrate-producing organism. Low = reduced colonocyte fuel, increased gut inflammation. Depleted by antibiotics, stress, low-fiber diet. Prebiotic fiber + resistant starch restores F. prausnitzii.
Bifidobacterium · Lactobacillus
Detected (adequate)
Core commensal bacteria. Depletion indicates dysbiosis, antibiotic damage, or poor dietary diversity. Support through multi-strain probiotics (25–50B CFU) after clearance phase.
Beta-Glucuronidase
< 365 U/mL
Elevated = dysbiotic bacteria reactivating deconjugated estrogens → estrogen recirculation. Key in hormone-sensitive conditions (estrogen dominance, breast health). Calcium D-Glucarate 1.5g/day inhibits this enzyme.
GI-MAP — Gut Immune & Permeability Markers
Secretory IgA (SIgA)
510–2,010 mcg/mL
Front-line mucosal immune defense. Low = compromised gut immunity, susceptibility to recurrent infections and dysbiosis. High = acute immune activation or stress response. Colostrum, Vitamin D, and zinc support SIgA production.
Calprotectin
< 50 mcg/g
GI inflammation marker. Released by neutrophils. Elevated in IBD (Crohn's, UC), infection, active dysbiosis. Differentiates true inflammatory bowel disease from functional/IBS presentation.
Zonulin
< 107 ng/mL
Intestinal permeability marker (leaky gut). Produced by enterocytes in response to gluten, dysbiosis, and stress. Elevated = tight junctions opening. Most useful as a directional marker — not a precise quantifier.
Anti-Gliadin IgA
Not elevated
Immune response to gliadin (gluten protein) in the gut mucosa. Elevated = gut immune activation to gluten — even if celiac disease blood markers (tTG-IgA) are negative. Important in the leaky gut picture.
Elastase-1 (Pancreatic)
> 200 mcg/g
Pancreatic exocrine function marker. Low = pancreatic enzyme insufficiency — fat malabsorption, nutrient depletion. Gut dysbiosis can secondarily impair pancreatic output. Supplement with pancreatic enzymes if low.
GI Effects™ Fundamentals (Genova Diagnostics) — Culture + Metabolites Stool Test

Stool test. Culture-based (not PCR) — grows organisms in lab to identify and test sensitivity. Adds digestion/absorption markers, short-chain fatty acid metabolites, and antimicrobial sensitivity testing not available on GI-MAP. Use when you want organism sensitivity data or richer metabolic picture.

When to choose GI Effects over GI-MAP: (1) You want to know which natural agents (Berberine, Oregano, Caprylic Acid) will specifically inhibit the Candida or bacteria isolated from THIS patient — GI Effects tests this directly. (2) You want digestion markers (Pancreatic Elastase, Fecal Fat, SCFA breakdown). (3) You want to confirm and quantify butyrate production. GI-MAP is superior for H. pylori virulence/resistance and pathogen quantification. Ideal: both tests together.
GI Effects — Digestion & Absorption Markers
Pancreatic Elastase 1
> 200 mcg/g
Gold-standard non-invasive marker for pancreatic exocrine function. Low = pancreatic enzyme insufficiency → fat malabsorption → nutrient depletion. Values below 100 = severe insufficiency requiring enzyme replacement. Normal is >200 mcg/g.
Fecal Fat (Total)
3.2–38.6 mg/g
Total fat in stool — includes triglycerides, long-chain fatty acids, cholesterol, phospholipids. Elevated = fat malabsorption from pancreatic, biliary, or intestinal dysfunction. Component breakdown distinguishes the source.
Triglycerides
0.3–2.8 mg/g
Elevated stool triglycerides specifically suggest pancreatic lipase deficiency — undigested dietary fats passing through intact.
Long-Chain Fatty Acids
1.2–29.1 mg/g

Standard Labs — Gut-Relevant (Order Through Any Physician)

These labs are covered by most insurance plans and available through any standard lab (Quest, Labcorp). They provide the essential context for gut health and are the foundation before adding functional testing.

Standard Labs — Liver & Bile Panel

The liver produces bile acids. Bile flow is essential for fat digestion, toxin elimination, gut motility, and antifungal defense. Gut dysbiosis — especially Candida and mold — stresses liver bile production. These markers tell you how well the bile system is functioning.

Total Bilirubin
0.1–1.2 mg/dL
Optimal: 0.4–1.0
Overall bile clearance indicator. Elevated = liver congestion, hemolysis, or bile duct obstruction. Low = insufficient bile acid production. Bilirubin is also a potent antioxidant — very low levels may reflect oxidative stress.
Direct (Conjugated) Bilirubin
0.0–0.3 mg/dL
Bilirubin that has been processed by the liver (conjugated). Elevated direct bilirubin = liver is processing but can't excrete into bile (intrahepatic obstruction, hepatocellular damage). Key for distinguishing liver vs. pre-hepatic cause.
Indirect (Unconjugated) Bilirubin
0.1–0.9 mg/dL
Bilirubin not yet processed by the liver. Elevated indirect = excessive red blood cell breakdown (hemolysis) or impaired liver conjugation. Also elevated in Gilbert's syndrome — a benign variant that can make bilirubin interpretation complex.
ALT (SGPT)
7–40 U/L
Functional optimal: <25
Liver cell damage marker. ALT is liver-specific. Elevation signals hepatocyte stress — from antifungal medications (monitor with Itraconazole), mold toxins, or NAFLD. Trend over time matters more than single values.
AST (SGOT)
10–40 U/L
Functional optimal: <25
Liver and muscle damage marker (less liver-specific than ALT). AST:ALT ratio above 2:1 suggests alcoholic liver disease. High AST with normal ALT = muscle source (check CK). Elevated during intensive exercise — context matters.
GGT (Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase)
9–48 U/L
Optimal: <30 (men), <20 (women)
Most sensitive marker for bile duct stress, alcohol exposure, and hepatic toxin load. Elevated GGT with normal ALT/AST = often toxic or biliary origin — think mycotoxins, alcohol, or medication exposure. A key early warning marker in mold cases.
Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)
44–147 U/L
Optimal: 50–90
Bile duct marker and bone marker. Elevated = biliary obstruction (gallstones, bile sludge), liver disease, or rapid bone turnover. Isolated ALP elevation with normal bilirubin often = biliary congestion without full obstruction.
Albumin
3.5–5.0 g/dL
Optimal: >4.0
Liver synthesis marker and nutritional protein status. Albumin takes weeks to change — it's not an acute marker. Low albumin = malnutrition, malabsorption, or chronic liver insufficiency. Critical in leaky gut patients with multiple food sensitivities and nutrient depletion.

hsCRP (high-sensitivity CRP)
< 1.0 mg/L
Optimal: <0.5 mg/L
Systemic inflammation signal. Gut dysbiosis, leaky gut, and ongoing infection all drive CRP elevation. As gut healing progresses, hsCRP should drop. Values above 3.0 mg/L are associated with significant cardiovascular and chronic disease risk.
Ferritin
12–300 ng/mL (men)
12–150 ng/mL (women)
Functional optimal: 50–150
Iron storage protein AND acute-phase reactant. Ferritin rises in inflammation even when iron stores are low — masking iron deficiency in the presence of gut inflammation. Very low ferritin (<20) in gut dysbiosis often reflects true iron depletion from malabsorption.
ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate)
< 20 mm/hr (<50 yrs)
< 30 mm/hr (>50 yrs)
Non-specific systemic inflammation marker. Slower to respond than CRP — useful for tracking chronic inflammatory processes over weeks. Elevated in autoimmune conditions, chronic infections, and ongoing dysbiosis-driven inflammation.
Homocysteine
5–15 µmol/L
Optimal: <8 µmol/L
Methylation and B-vitamin status marker. Elevated homocysteine = B12, B6, or folate insufficiency, or MTHFR variant expression. Gut dysbiosis impairs B12 absorption → elevated homocysteine. Also an independent cardiovascular risk marker. Treat with methylcobalamin + methylfolate + P5P.

30–100 ng/mL

200–900 pg/mL

Zinc (Serum)
70–120 mcg/dL
Gut lining integrity, wound healing, immune function. Depleted by Candida, gut inflammation, and malabsorption. Serum zinc is a rough proxy — RBC zinc is more accurate but less commonly available. Supplement with zinc picolinate or bisglycinate for best absorption.

4.2–6.8 mg/dL

Antifungals
Itraconazole
200mg once or twice daily · Oral capsule · 8–12 weeks
Best for: Aspergillus (mold) in gut. Also effective for Candida. Pharmaceutical-grade required — herbal antifungals are insufficient for Aspergillus species. Pulsed dosing for sensitive patients (2 weeks on, 1 week off).
WHEN TO USE: Multiple Aspergillus markers elevated on OAT (Tartaric, 5-OH-methylfuroic, Furan-2,5-dicarboxylic). Mold exposure history. Concurrent TUDCA + binders essential. Monitor liver enzymes (LFTs) at baseline and 4–6 weeks.
Nystatin
500,000–1,000,000 IU · 2–4x daily · Oral · 4–8 weeks
Best for: Intestinal Candida (non-systemic). Nystatin is not absorbed — it acts locally in the GI tract. Minimal systemic side effects. Good first-line option for straightforward Candida without mold burden.
WHEN TO USE: Arabinose elevated, Aspergillus markers normal. No mold exposure history. Well-tolerated by sensitive patients. Often used with herbal adjuncts (caprylic acid, berberine).
Fluconazole
100–200mg daily · OR 150mg weekly · Oral · 4–8 weeks
Best for: Systemic Candida, recurrent vaginal Candida, esophageal Candida. Systemic absorption — broader coverage than Nystatin. Not effective for Aspergillus (Aspergillus is inherently resistant).
WHEN TO USE: Moderate–severe Arabinose on OAT, or confirmed systemic Candida. Monitor LFTs with extended use. Drug interactions with many CYP3A4 substrates.
SIBO Antibiotics
Rifaximin (Xifaxan)
550mg · 3x daily · 14 days
Best for: Hydrogen-dominant SIBO. Non-absorbed antibiotic — acts locally in small intestine with minimal systemic effects. Excellent safety profile. Reduces hydrogen-producing bacteria (mostly Gram-negative).
WHEN TO USE: SIBO breath test positive for hydrogen. Upper bloating within 1–2 hours of eating, belching. May require 2 courses for complete resolution. Consider herbal alternative if cost is prohibitive.
Rifaximin + Neomycin
Rifaximin 550mg + Neomycin 500mg · 3x daily · 14 days
Best for: Methane-dominant SIBO (IMO — Intestinal Methanogen Overgrowth). Neomycin targets Archaea (methane-producing organisms) that Rifaximin alone doesn't fully address. Constipation-predominant presentation.
WHEN TO USE: Methane >10 ppm on breath test. Constipation-dominant bowel pattern. More intensive than hydrogen protocol — monitor for GI side effects of Neomycin.
H. Pylori Eradication
Triple Therapy (H. pylori)
PPI + Amoxicillin 1g + Clarithromycin 500mg · Twice daily · 14 days
First-line eradication. PPI (proton pump inhibitor) reduces stomach acid to allow antibiotics to work. Clarithromycin resistance is increasing — confirm no prior Clarithromycin use before selecting this regimen.
WHEN TO USE: H. pylori confirmed on GI-MAP or urea breath test. Symptomatic (epigastric pain, nausea, early satiety). Concurrent S. boulardii to reduce antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Retest at 4+ weeks post-treatment.
Bismuth Quadruple Therapy
Bismuth subsalicylate + Tetracycline + Metronidazole + PPI · 10–14 days
Second-line or Clarithromycin-resistant cases. Higher eradication rate than triple therapy when resistance is present. Bismuth has direct anti-H. pylori and mucosal-protective effects.
WHEN TO USE: Prior Clarithromycin exposure, prior triple therapy failure, or geographic area with high resistance. Heavier side effect profile — bismuth causes black stools (normal, not GI bleed).

Aha Moment 6 · Conventional Medicine Blind Spot
What if H. pylori's greatest damage has nothing to do with ulcers — and it starts decades before anyone tests for it?
H. pylori disrupts parietal cell function in the stomach — progressively destroying the cells that produce both hydrochloric acid AND intrinsic factor. Intrinsic factor is required for B12 absorption. Long-term H. pylori infection = progressive B12 depletion over years, often while stomach acid tests appear "normal." B12 deficiency presents as cognitive decline, peripheral neuropathy, elevated homocysteine, and cardiovascular risk — none of which will be connected to H. pylori in standard care. The ulcer is the least of it.
  • Citation: Kaptan K, et al. "Helicobacter pylori — is it a novel causative agent in Vitamin B12 deficiency?" Archives of Internal Medicine, 160(9):1349–1353, 2000. Also: Serin E, et al. Journal of Gastroenterology, 37(3):187–190, 2002.

Approximately 90% of the body's serotonin is synthesized in enterochromaffin cells of the gut epithelium — not in the brain. The gut microbiome directly regulates this production. Treating the gut changes the serotonin precursor supply — something no antidepressant does.
  • Citation: Yano JM, et al. "Indigenous bacteria from the gut microbiota regulate host serotonin biosynthesis." Cell, 161(2):264–276, 2015.
