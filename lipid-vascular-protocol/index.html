◆ The Assumption Worth Questioning

“Your cholesterol is high. We need to get that number down.”

Cholesterol is not a toxin your body reluctantly tolerates. It is structural material in every cell membrane you own, the raw material for every steroid hormone you make, and it is concentrated more densely in your brain than anywhere else in your body. Your liver manufactures most of it on purpose, every single day. So the useful question was never “is the number high?” The useful questions are: how many particles are carrying it, how long have they been circulating, and is the arterial wall they're passing healthy enough to keep them out? Those are three different questions, and only one of them is on a standard lab report. What if the number you've been watching for twenty years is the least informative thing on the page?

What this protocol is

This is a map of the entire lipid and vascular picture — not just the four numbers on a standard panel, but particle count, particle behavior, oxidation, inflammation, endothelial function, genetics, imaging, and the flow physics that decide where disease actually lands. It holds two things at once: cholesterol has been badly misrepresented as a villain, and the evidence that atherogenic particle burden drives arterial disease over decades is among the strongest in medicine. Both are true. Most of what you've read online picks one and pretends the other doesn't exist.

◆ The Core Reframe — Count Beats Cargo

Cholesterol cannot travel through blood on its own — it's a fat, and blood is water. So it rides inside particles: a phospholipid shell wrapped around a cholesterol and triglyceride core, addressed by a protein tag called ApoB. Every atherogenic particle carries exactly one ApoB, from the moment the liver releases it to the moment it's cleared.

That single fact is the hinge of this entire protocol. Measuring cholesterol tells you how much cargo is in circulation. Measuring ApoB tells you how many vehicles are on the road. Two people with identical LDL cholesterol can be carrying wildly different particle counts — and it is the particles, not the cargo, that lodge in the arterial wall.

Watch for this: the same distinction shows up three separate times in this protocol. ApoB beats LDL-C. Lp(a) in nmol/L beats mg/dL. Particle number beats particle size. Once you see the pattern, most of the confusion in the cholesterol conversation dissolves.

Every VLDL, IDL and LDL particle carries exactly one ApoB-100 molecule for its entire lifespan. LDL cholesterol measures the cargo. ApoB counts the vehicles.

How to read the evidence tiers

Not every marker in this protocol carries the same weight of evidence, and pretending otherwise is how trust gets lost. Every lab, therapy and mechanism here is tagged with one of three tiers. This tagging is the point. Discernment is not knowing more markers — it's knowing which ones have earned the right to change what you do.

Tier 1 markers have both genetic causal evidence and randomized outcome data. Tier 3 markers have real mechanisms and real epidemiology — but when drugs were built to move them, events didn't follow. Interesting is not the same as actionable.
◆ A Word About Numbers That Are Too Low

This protocol spends most of its time on excess. But cholesterol that runs too low carries its own cost, and almost nobody mentions it. Membrane cholesterol content directly affects serotonin receptor function — strip it out and you have fewer working receptors. Cholesterol is also the precursor to every steroid hormone you make, including the neurosteroids that regulate mood and stress resilience. Low serum cholesterol has been repeatedly associated with depression, and the association traces back to early cholesterol-lowering trials that showed an unexpected excess of deaths from suicide and violence. The commonly cited watch-point is a total cholesterol below roughly 160 mg/dL. Reverse causation applies here too — depression changes appetite and metabolism — but the mechanism is real. Lower is not infinitely better. Nothing in biology is.

#1
FOOD

Food sits at the top not because dietary cholesterol drives your serum cholesterol — for most people it barely does — but because food controls almost everything upstream of the particle. It sets triglyceride production and therefore VLDL output. It sets insulin sensitivity, which determines whether your particles run small and dense or large and buoyant. It supplies or starves the viscous fiber that pulls bile acids out of circulation and forces your liver to upregulate LDL receptors — the same mechanism a bile acid sequestrant drug uses. And it sets the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio that determines whether your cell membranes lean toward inflammation or toward resolution. Roughly 75–80% of your cholesterol is manufactured endogenously, but what you eat writes the instructions for that manufacturing.

◆ PROTOCOL-SPECIFIC ACTION
Get total viscous fiber to 25–40 g/day, anchored by 10 g psyllium husk daily in divided doses with a large glass of water. This single move reliably lowers ApoB and costs almost nothing.

Viscous fiber — the highest-leverage food intervention. Soluble, gel-forming fiber binds bile acids in the gut and carries them out in stool. Your liver, now short on bile acids, pulls cholesterol from circulation to make more — by upregulating LDL receptors on hepatocytes. This is mechanistically identical to how bile acid sequestrant drugs work. Targets: psyllium husk 10 g/day (start at 5 g and build to avoid bloating, always with generous water); oats and barley for beta-glucans 3–5 g/day; legumes and lentils 1–2 cups/day; apples, pears and citrus for pectin; chia and ground flax 2 tbsp/day. Total viscous fiber 25–40 g/day. Expect roughly a 6–9% LDL-C reduction from fiber alone when done consistently.

Saturated fat — the honest version. Saturated fat raises LDL-C and ApoB in most people, and the size of that response varies enormously between individuals. This is not a moral question, it's a phenotype question. If you carry an APOE e4 allele, or you're a hyperabsorber, your response to saturated fat and dietary cholesterol is substantially larger than average. If you're an e2 carrier or a hyperproducer, it may be minimal. You cannot know which you are without testing.

And an equally honest counterweight: these are not junk foods. Real butter, properly made coconut oil, well-raised meat and quality cheese each carry nutrients you will struggle to get elsewhere, and reducing them is a targeted experiment for a specific phenotype — not a moral verdict on the food.

  • Butter, especially pasture-raised — one of the better dietary sources of vitamin K2 (as MK-4), which activates matrix Gla protein and helps direct calcium into bone rather than artery wall. Also butyrate (the same short-chain fatty acid your gut bacteria make from fiber, and the preferred fuel of colon cells), conjugated linoleic acid, and fat-soluble vitamins A, D and E. Grass-fed butter carries meaningfully more K2, CLA and omega-3 than grain-fed. Ghee is butter with the milk solids removed — concentrated in those same fat-soluble nutrients, free of lactose and casein, and far more heat-stable.
  • Coconut oil — but the processing matters enormously. Virgin, cold-pressed, unrefined coconut oil retains polyphenols and its characteristic aroma, and supplies medium-chain triglycerides and lauric acid, which has genuine antimicrobial activity and is absorbed and metabolised differently from long-chain fats. Refined, bleached and deodorised coconut oil — often labelled “RBD” — has been stripped of the polyphenols and heat-treated. Nutritionally these are close to two different foods wearing the same name.
  • Quality cheese — aged and traditionally fermented cheeses are among the richest food sources of vitamin K2 (as MK-7 and MK-9, produced by the bacteria doing the fermenting), plus calcium, CLA, and bioactive peptides generated during ageing. There is also a well-documented observation worth knowing: cheese consistently raises LDL less than its saturated fat content predicts. The leading explanations are that its calcium binds fatty acids in the gut to form insoluble soaps that are excreted rather than absorbed, and that the fermented dairy matrix itself changes how the fat is handled. Raw-milk and traditionally aged cheeses differ substantially from processed cheese product, which is a different category of food entirely.
  • Well-raised meat — pasture-raised and grass-finished meat carries more omega-3, more CLA, and more of several micronutrients than feedlot meat, along with heme iron, B12, zinc, taurine, creatine and carnosine. The distinction this protocol actually cares about is processed versus unprocessed: the epidemiological signal against “red meat” is consistently much stronger for processed meats — industrial deli meats, hot dogs, bacon cured with nitrites and cooked at high heat — than for unprocessed meat. Those are not the same exposure and should not carry the same recommendation.

Does what you eat it with change the outcome? Almost certainly — and this is genuinely underexplored. Fat is not absorbed in isolation. Calcium in dairy binds fatty acids into soaps that pass through rather than being absorbed. Viscous fiber eaten in the same meal binds bile acids, which are the emulsifiers required to absorb fat in the first place, and slows the whole process. And your gut microbiome sits in the middle of all of it — bacterial species determine how bile acids are deconjugated and recycled, how much short-chain fatty acid is produced, and how intestinal cells respond. That is very likely part of why two people eating identical amounts of the same saturated fat can show meaningfully different ApoB responses. Evidence tier 2 — the mechanisms are real and measurable, the individual prediction isn't reliable yet. Practically, it argues for eating these fats inside whole meals with fiber and fermented foods rather than in isolation, and it argues strongly for testing yourself rather than trusting a population average.

The practical experiment: if your ApoB is high and you want to know whether saturated fat is your lever, reduce it deliberately for 8–12 weeks — keeping quality high in whatever you retain — then retest ApoB. Let your own physiology answer the question rather than an internet argument. Some people will see a large drop. Some will see almost nothing. Both results are useful, and only one of them is knowable in advance, and only by testing.

Industrial seed oils and the omega ratio. The problem is not that omega-6 fats are toxic — arachidonic acid derived eicosanoids are exactly what you need when injured or infected. The problem is a chronic ratio that keeps the inflammatory arm dominant with too little EPA present to actively resolve it. Ancestral ratios ran roughly 1:1 to 4:1; modern Western intake runs 15:1 to 20:1 or higher.

But “seed oils are bad” collapses three separate arguments into one, and they do not stand or fall together. Separating them is the difference between a slogan and a decision you can act on.

Olive oil — and why most of the benefit isn't the fat. Extra-virgin olive oil is genuinely excellent, but the reason is often misattributed. Its monounsaturated fat is unremarkable; oleic acid is available from many sources. What makes EVOO distinctive is its polyphenols — oleocanthal, hydroxytyrosol and oleuropein — which reduce LDL oxidation, improve HDL function and carry real anti-inflammatory activity. Oleocanthal in particular inhibits the same COX enzymes that ibuprofen does. And you can taste it. That peppery, stinging burn at the back of the throat that makes you cough is oleocanthal, and it is the single best kitchen test of whether your oil is the real thing. A smooth, bland, buttery olive oil is a weak olive oil — it has been refined, over-filtered, or has sat too long. Buy dark glass, look for a harvest date rather than a best-before date, and use it within months of opening.

Which fat for which job — smoke point matters. Making EVOO your universal cooking fat is a common recommendation and not quite the right one, because heat is exactly what destroys the polyphenols you're paying for. Match the fat to the task:

  • Raw, finishing, dressings, low heat: extra-virgin olive oil. This is where its polyphenols survive and where its value actually lives.
  • Medium to high heat: ghee (very high smoke point, and the milk solids that would otherwise burn have been removed), quality tallow or duck fat, or avocado oil. One warning on avocado oil — independent testing has repeatedly found a large share of retail bottles to be rancid or cut with cheaper oils, so brand matters more here than for almost any other product in your kitchen.
  • Any fat, any temperature: if it is smoking in the pan, it has passed its useful point and is generating oxidation products. Turn the heat down or change the fat.

Sugar, fructose and triglycerides. Fructose is metabolised almost entirely in the liver and is a direct substrate for de novo lipogenesis — it drives triglyceride synthesis, VLDL export, and therefore remnant cholesterol and small dense LDL. This is the fastest route to the classic atherogenic triad of high triglycerides, low HDL and small dense particles.

◆ But Nobody Ever Got Diabetes From Eating Too Much Fruit

And that observation is correct. The difference is dose and rate, not the molecule.

Your liver's capacity to process fructose has a throughput limit. Delivered slowly, it is handled without difficulty. Delivered fast, it overflows into de novo lipogenesis. An apple is roughly 10 g of fructose bound inside a fiber matrix with water, potassium, magnesium and polyphenols, eaten over several minutes, with enough satiety that you are unlikely to want a fourth one. A 20-ounce soda is about 35 g in liquid form, with no fiber to slow it, no chewing, no satiety signal, and rapid delivery straight to the liver. Same molecule. Completely different physiological event.

The epidemiology follows exactly that split: whole fruit intake is consistently associated with lower diabetes and cardiovascular risk, while fruit juice tracks with higher risk. Same sugar, different vehicle. And high-fructose corn syrup versus table sugar is only a marginal compositional difference — roughly 55% versus 50% fructose. What HFCS actually changed was economics: it made cheap sweetening ubiquitous, and overwhelmingly in liquid form.

Two honest caveats. Dried fruit and juice behave much more like the beverage than like the fruit — the fiber matrix is gone or the water is, and the dose per mouthful climbs steeply. And someone with established fatty liver or significant insulin resistance may need to moderate even whole fruit temporarily while the terrain is being repaired. For nearly everyone else, whole fruit is not the problem, and treating it as though it were is a good way to make someone avoid one of the better foods available to them.

Eliminate sugar-sweetened beverages first — they are the single largest source and the one with the clearest mechanism.

The overall pattern. A Mediterranean-style pattern built on extra-virgin olive oil, fatty fish, legumes, nuts, abundant vegetables, berries and modest whole grains has the strongest outcome evidence of any dietary pattern for cardiovascular endpoints. Layer on: lycopene from cooked tomatoes, raw cocoa and dark chocolate above 80%, green tea, garlic, and berries for polyphenols.

Two thousand calories is not two thousand calories. Over a few days, a calorie really is close to a calorie — energy balance is real physics and it governs short-term weight. But this protocol is about decades, and over decades the two diverge sharply, because food is not only fuel. It is information and it is raw material. Two thousand calories of whole food supplies the magnesium and potassium your vessels need for tone, the fiber that feeds the bacteria that make butyrate, the polyphenols that protect your particles from oxidation, the choline and B vitamins your methylation cycle runs on, and the protein and minerals your body rebuilds itself from. Two thousand calories of refined flour, industrial oil and added sugar supplies energy and very little else — while actively driving insulin upward, triglycerides upward, and the small dense particle pattern this whole protocol is built to prevent. The machinery does not fail on day three. It fails on year fifteen, quietly, and gets called aging. Same number on the label, radically different trajectory.

Meal timing. Time-restricted eating in an 8–10 hour window improves insulin sensitivity and triglycerides independently of calorie change in many people. Eat earlier rather than later — late-night eating collides with circadian metabolic physiology and worsens post-meal lipid and glucose handling.

#2
MOVEMENT

Movement earns second place in this protocol because it is the only intervention that acts on three separate mechanisms simultaneously, two of which almost nobody discusses. It improves insulin sensitivity, which shifts your particle profile from small-dense toward large-buoyant. It raises systemic shear stress across the entire arterial tree, which switches endothelial cells into their athero-protective gene program — more nitric oxide, less inflammatory signaling, a better-maintained glycocalyx. And because lymphatic vessels have no central pump, lymph flow depends on the vessels' own rhythmic contractions assisted by skeletal muscle contraction and respiratory pressure swings — and that is the route cholesterol uses to exit the arterial wall. Sedentary behavior isn't merely correlated with vascular disease. It removes the largest external assist from the drain.

◆ PROTOCOL-SPECIFIC ACTION
150–180 minutes/week of Zone 2 (e.g., brisk walk where you can hold a full conversation, easy cycling, light swimming, conversational-pace jog) plus 2–3 resistance sessions. Then add the piece most people skip: stand and move for 2–3 minutes every 30–45 minutes of sitting.

Zone 2 — the aerobic base. Zone 2 is the intensity at which you're working steadily but could still hold a full conversation — roughly 60–70% of max heart rate, at or just below the first lactate threshold. Examples: a brisk walk where you can talk in complete sentences, easy cycling, light swimming, a conversational-pace jog, rucking on flat ground. Target 150–180 minutes/week across 3–5 sessions of 30–60 minutes. Zone 2 preferentially builds mitochondrial density and fat oxidation capacity, which is what actually moves triglycerides and insulin sensitivity.

Resistance training. 2–3 sessions/week covering the major compound movements — squat or leg press, hinge or deadlift, horizontal push and pull, vertical push and pull, loaded carry. Muscle is your largest glucose disposal organ; more of it means less circulating insulin, which means less VLDL output and fewer small dense particles. Resistance work also produces the most powerful skeletal muscle pump for lymphatic return.

VO2 max work. One session per week of higher-intensity intervals — classic protocol is 4 minutes hard, 3 minutes easy, repeated 4 times. Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest single predictors of all-cause mortality in the literature, stronger than most lipid markers taken alone.

The sitting problem is its own problem. Prolonged sitting produces measurable declines in leg endothelial function within hours, driven by reduced blood flow and shear stress. Critically, this is not fully offset by a workout earlier in the day — the exposure and the antidote operate on different timescales. Interrupt sitting every 30–45 minutes with 2–3 minutes of movement. Calf raises, a lap of the house, a flight of stairs. This is a shear stress intervention and a lymphatic intervention at the same time.

Lymphatic-specific movement. Because lymph flow is assisted by muscle contraction and respiratory pressure changes, a few modalities punch above their weight: rebounding on a mini-trampoline, walking, swimming (hydrostatic pressure assists venous and lymphatic return), inversions or simply elevating the legs, and manual lymphatic drainage or dry brushing toward the heart. Evidence tier 2 — strong mechanism, no cardiovascular outcome trials.

What to expect. Exercise reliably lowers triglycerides and improves HDL function and insulin sensitivity. It moves LDL-C and ApoB less than people hope — often modestly. That's not failure. The endothelial and lymphatic benefits don't show up on a lipid panel at all, and they may matter more than the number that does.

#3
SLEEP & LIGHT

Sleep is where the metabolic terrain that produces atherogenic dyslipidemia is either repaired or degraded. A single week of restricted sleep measurably worsens insulin sensitivity in healthy adults — and insulin resistance is the engine that drives high triglycerides, low HDL, and small dense LDL. Untreated obstructive sleep apnea adds a second mechanism entirely: repeated nocturnal hypoxia and reoxygenation is a potent oxidative and sympathetic stressor that damages endothelium and drives hypertension directly. In this protocol, sleep apnea is the single most commonly missed reversible cause of a stubbornly bad vascular picture. If someone snores, wakes unrefreshed, or has a thick neck and morning headaches, that is the thread to pull before adjusting anything else.

◆ PROTOCOL-SPECIFIC ACTION
Screen for sleep apnea before blaming your diet. Then anchor the circadian clock: 10–20 minutes of outdoor light within 60 minutes of waking, every day, no sunglasses.

Duration and consistency. Target 7–9 hours with a consistent sleep and wake time — consistency matters nearly as much as duration, because an irregular schedule is itself a circadian stressor. Both short sleep and markedly long sleep associate with worse cardiovascular outcomes, though the long-sleep end is heavily confounded by underlying illness.

Sleep apnea screening. Red flags: snoring, witnessed apneas, waking unrefreshed, morning headache, nocturia, resistant hypertension, atrial fibrillation, neck circumference above 17 inches in men or 16 in women. Screening questionnaires (STOP-BANG, Berlin) are a reasonable first pass; home sleep testing confirms. Treating moderate-to-severe apnea improves blood pressure and endothelial function measurably. Note honestly: outcome trials of CPAP for hard cardiovascular endpoints have been mixed, largely due to poor adherence.

Light as the master signal. Morning outdoor light — even on an overcast day, which still delivers far more lux than indoor lighting — entrains the suprachiasmatic nucleus and sets the timing of the entire metabolic day, including lipid handling and glucose tolerance. Get 10–20 minutes within an hour of waking. Then protect the other end: dim household lighting after sunset, reduce blue-wavelength exposure in the 2–3 hours before bed, and keep the bedroom fully dark. Nocturnal light exposure suppresses melatonin, which is not only a sleep signal but a meaningful antioxidant with direct vascular effects.

Shift work and circadian mismatch. Rotating shift work is an independent cardiovascular risk factor and worsens the entire lipid profile. If it can't be avoided, the mitigations are aggressive light management, fixed rather than rotating schedules where possible, and strict eating windows aligned to the working day rather than grazing across the night.

Practical stack. Cool room (65–68°F), blackout, no alcohol within 3 hours of bed (it fragments the second half of the night and suppresses REM), caffeine cutoff 8–10 hours before sleep, and a wind-down routine that reliably drops sympathetic tone.

#4
ELECTRICAL

Every heartbeat is an electrical event before it is a mechanical one, and every endothelial cell holds a membrane potential it must actively maintain. The vascular relevance here is real: red blood cells carry a negative surface charge that keeps them repelling one another rather than clumping, and that charge relationship shapes blood viscosity and microcirculatory flow. Grounding, red and near-infrared light, and PEMF all sit in this pillar as ways of supporting mitochondrial charge and vascular tone. Be honest about the tiers — photobiomodulation has the best support of the three, grounding has small human studies suggesting effects on blood viscosity and heart rate variability, and PEMF for cardiovascular endpoints specifically remains thin.

Red and near-infrared light (photobiomodulation). Wavelengths in the 630–680 nm and 800–880 nm ranges are absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, improving ATP output and releasing nitric oxide bound at that site. The nitric oxide release is the vascular story — local vasodilation and improved microcirculation. Practical use: 10–20 minutes, 3–5x/week, at a distance the device specifies. Evidence tier 2.

Grounding / earthing. Direct skin contact with the earth — barefoot on grass, sand or soil for 20–30 minutes — has been studied in small trials showing reduced blood viscosity, improved zeta potential (the surface charge keeping red cells apart), and shifts in heart rate variability toward parasympathetic dominance. The mechanism is plausible and the intervention is free and risk-free. The evidence base is small and largely from a handful of investigator groups. Evidence tier 3, worth doing anyway because the downside is zero.

PEMF. Pulsed electromagnetic field therapy has reasonable evidence for bone healing and pain, and much thinner evidence for vascular endpoints. Listed for completeness, not prioritized here.

EMF hygiene. The honest position: high-quality human evidence connecting typical household EMF exposure to cardiovascular outcomes is lacking. Low-cost precautions — not sleeping with a phone at the pillow, wired connections where convenient, airplane mode overnight — cost nothing and reduce a variable some members care about. Nothing in this protocol depends on them.

#5
WATER

Blood is mostly water, and its viscosity is a genuine and underappreciated hemodynamic variable — thicker blood means higher shear resistance, greater cardiac work, and poorer microcirculatory perfusion. Chronic low-grade dehydration raises hematocrit and viscosity measurably. Mineral content matters as much as volume: magnesium and potassium are direct determinants of vascular tone and endothelial function, and both are widely under-consumed. There is also a genuinely interesting frontier here — water adjacent to hydrophilic surfaces forms an ordered, charge-separated layer that excludes solutes, a real and reproducible laboratory phenomenon. Whether that structured layer plays a meaningful role along the vessel wall in living humans is unproven and should be held as an open question, not a claim.

Volume and timing. Roughly half your body weight in ounces daily as a starting point, adjusted for activity, heat and caffeine. Front-load earlier in the day. Morning dehydration after a night's fast is one contributor to the well-documented morning clustering of cardiovascular events.

Minerals, not just water. Magnesium is a direct vasodilator and a cofactor in hundreds of enzymatic reactions including those governing endothelial nitric oxide production; RBC magnesium is the meaningful test, serum magnesium is nearly useless. Potassium opposes sodium's pressor effect and is chronically under-consumed on a modern diet. Adequate but not excessive sodium — both very low and very high intakes associate with worse outcomes, and the reflexive “lower is always better” message oversimplifies a J-shaped relationship. Natural mineral water, a pinch of unrefined salt in water, or added trace minerals all work.

The structured water question, held honestly. Laboratory work has repeatedly demonstrated that water next to certain hydrophilic surfaces organizes into a layer that excludes solutes and carries a net charge, energized in part by infrared radiation. That much is published and reproducible. The larger extrapolation — that this layer forms extensively along the vascular endothelium and contributes meaningfully to blood propulsion — has not been demonstrated in living humans and does not yet have a quantitative model matching the pressure and flow measurements taken in patients every day. It belongs in this protocol as a compelling open question about the microcirculation, not as an established mechanism. Practical, no-cost moves in the meantime: favor fresh spring or glacial water, get morning sunlight on skin, and eat water-dense whole plants.

What actually matters most here. Filtration to remove chlorine, chloramine, heavy metals and microplastics is the highest-value water intervention with the least controversy attached.

#6
AIR

Air ranks sixth by convention but carries two mechanisms in this protocol that punch well above that placement. First: the paranasal sinuses continuously produce nitric oxide, which is inhaled with every nasal breath and delivered straight to the pulmonary and systemic circulation — nasal breathing is literally a nitric oxide delivery system, and mouth breathing bypasses it entirely. Second: lymph has no central pump. Collecting lymphatic vessels contract rhythmically on their own, and that intrinsic pumping is powerfully assisted by skeletal muscle contraction and by the negative intrathoracic pressure generated by deep diaphragmatic breathing. The thoracic duct — where lymph from most of the body, including the arterial wall, finally empties back into the bloodstream — drains under that respiratory pressure gradient. Shallow chest breathing all day means a lymphatic system running on one cylinder.

Nasal breathing, always. Nitric oxide generated in the paranasal sinuses is carried into the airways and lungs on inhaled air, improving ventilation-perfusion matching and contributing to systemic NO availability. It is also antimicrobial. Nasal breathing further slows respiratory rate and increases CO2 tolerance, which improves oxygen offloading at the tissue via the Bohr effect. Practical: nasal breathe during all Zone 2 work; consider mouth taping at night if you wake with a dry mouth, provided sleep apnea has been ruled out first.

Diaphragmatic breathing as a lymphatic pump. Deep diaphragmatic breaths create larger swings in intrathoracic pressure, which draws lymph up the thoracic duct. Practical protocol: 5–10 minutes daily of slow diaphragmatic breathing at roughly 5–6 breaths per minute — inhale 4–5 seconds through the nose, exhale 6–7 seconds. This same cadence sits near the resonance frequency that maximizes heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity, so you get autonomic benefit and lymphatic benefit from one practice.

Slow breathing and blood pressure. Device-guided slow breathing has enough evidence for blood pressure reduction that it has been reviewed favorably in hypertension literature. Free versions work: box breathing, 4-7-8, coherent breathing at 5.5 breaths/minute.

Air quality is a cardiovascular variable, not just a respiratory one. Fine particulate exposure (PM2.5) is a well-established driver of cardiovascular events and accelerates atherosclerosis through systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. It is one of the more consistently replicated environmental risk factors in the entire field. Practical: HEPA filtration in the bedroom and main living space, avoid exercising alongside heavy traffic, address indoor sources including gas stoves, candles, mold and off-gassing furnishings.

#7
SPIRIT & PURPOSE

Whether that's God, your higher self, a deep belief in yourself, or simply what gets you out of bed before the alarm — this is your animating force. It ranks seventh here only because the mechanisms above are more directly measurable, not because it matters less. Psychosocial stress carried a population-attributable risk in the INTERHEART study comparable to hypertension and abdominal obesity, and purpose in life has been independently associated with lower cardiovascular event rates across multiple cohorts. The physiology is not mysterious: chronic sympathetic activation raises blood pressure and heart rate, drives cortisol and therefore insulin resistance and visceral fat, increases platelet reactivity, and degrades endothelial function. Meaning is not a soft variable here. It is an autonomic input.

The distinction that matters most in this entire protocol. Cumulative particle burden builds the substrate over decades. Acute stress often supplies the trigger — the catecholamine surge, the blood pressure spike, the platelet activation and coronary vasoconstriction that ruptures a plaque which was already sitting there. Stress determines when. Cholesterol determines whether there was something there to rupture. Both are true, they answer different questions, and most arguments about heart disease are two people answering different questions at each other.

Chronic load versus acute spike. Chronic stress works slowly through cortisol, visceral adiposity, insulin resistance and sleep disruption — it builds substrate. Acute stress works in minutes through catecholamines — it pulls triggers. A person carrying years of chronic load has both more substrate and a lower threshold for the trigger, which is why the combination is so much more dangerous than either alone.

Practical levers. Daily practice of some kind — prayer, meditation, contemplative reading, breathwork — that reliably drops sympathetic tone. Time in nature. Purpose expressed as concrete commitments rather than abstraction: who is counting on you, what are you building, what would be left unfinished. Cultivate the ability to recover after activation, not the fantasy of never being activated.

#8
COMMUNITY & CONNECTION

The most underestimated pillar, and the one members are most likely to skip past on their way to the supplement list. Social isolation and loneliness have been associated with increased risk of coronary heart disease and stroke in large meta-analyses, with effect sizes that sit in the same range as several conventional risk factors. The pathways are the ones already described in this protocol — chronically elevated sympathetic tone, higher inflammatory markers including IL-6 and CRP, worse sleep, worse health behaviors, and blunted recovery from stress. Connection is not the emotional garnish on a cardiovascular protocol. It is upstream of the inflammatory pathway that CANTOS proved was causal.

Quality over quantity. The protective signal tracks with perceived connection and the presence of people you can genuinely rely on, not with the size of a contact list. Someone can be surrounded and still isolated.

Marriage, caregiving and grief. Bereavement produces a sharp, measurable, transient spike in cardiovascular event risk in the days to weeks after a loss — the clearest possible demonstration that emotional state and cardiac events are physically linked. Caregiver strain works more slowly through the chronic pathway.

Practical. Regular in-person contact with people who know your history. Shared physical activity, which stacks two pillars at once. Service or contribution to something beyond yourself, which also feeds Spirit & Purpose. Structured community — faith communities, groups with a real cadence — outperforms good intentions, because it removes the need to decide.

◆ The Size Versus Count Debate, Presented Fairly

The count-first position: once you control for total particle number, the small-versus-large distinction largely washes out. Size correlates with count across a population, which is why it looked independently predictive — but the causal work is being done by how many particles are entering the wall, not how big each one is. On this view, particle size is a useful clue about someone's metabolic state and a poor treatment target.

The quality-matters position: small dense particles genuinely behave differently — longer residence time in circulation, easier penetration of the arterial wall, greater susceptibility to oxidation, less antioxidant content per particle. Counting particles may capture much of this downstream, but treating size as merely a marker understates real biological differences.

Where this protocol lands: both can be true simultaneously. Count carries more causal weight and is the better treatment target. Size and oxidation status tell you what kind of problem you have and therefore which lever to pull — a Pattern B profile points at insulin resistance and food, not at a statin. Use count to judge severity, use size to choose strategy.

◆ Why Lp(a) Comes Back In Two Different Units — And Why It Matters

One lab reports Lp(a) as 34 mg/dL. Another reports 96 nmol/L. These do not convert cleanly, and the reason is the single most important idea in this entire protocol showing up for the third time.

mg/dL measures mass. nmol/L measures particle number. The apo(a) protein varies enormously in size between individuals depending on how many kringle repeats they inherited — so a person with large isoforms carries more mass per particle than someone with small ones. Converting between the units drifts somewhere around 2.0 to 2.5 times and is individually imprecise.

Which means: nmol/L is the better unit for exactly the same reason ApoB is better than LDL-C, and particle number is better than particle size. Count beats cargo. Three separate places on one lab report. Ask for nmol/L when you can.

◆ How Often To Test Lp(a)

Lp(a) is 70–90% genetically determined and essentially fixed by adulthood, so most guidance calls it a once-in-a-lifetime test. This protocol treats it as baseline once, then recheck if you make a serious attempt at moving it — because it isn't completely immovable. Nicotinic acid lowers it modestly, and aggressive lifestyle change nudges it. Set expectations honestly at the start: nobody normalises a genetically high Lp(a) through lifestyle. Knowing the number changes how aggressively you drive every other lever, which is the actual reason to test it. One more interpretation note — average Lp(a) differs meaningfully by ancestry, running higher on average in people of African descent, and most labs still apply a single cutoff to everyone.

◆ Four Real Shortcomings Of The Standard Whole-Blood Omega Panel

1. Wrong compartment. The standard Quest panel measures whole blood. The validated research standard — the one all the sudden-cardiac-death outcome data was built on — is specifically red blood cell membrane. RBC membrane reflects roughly 120 days of tissue incorporation. Whole blood is meaningfully moved by what you ate yesterday. A salmon dinner the night before can shift a whole-blood result. It cannot shift an RBC result.

2. Different metric, same-looking number. The whole-blood panel reports EPA + DPA + DHA. The true Omega-3 Index is EPA + DHA only. That's why one has a cutoff of 5.5% and the other 8%. They are not interchangeable. Reading a whole-blood 5.1 as though it were an Omega-3 Index of 5.1 is a category error — and a common one.

3. Reference ranges wide enough to be meaningless. An arachidonic-acid-to-EPA ratio of 29.2 gets reported in plain black text as normal, inside a range of 3.7 to 40.7. A range that wide describes what a modern Western population looks like. It does not describe health.

4. Missing markers. No trans fatty acid index. No odd-chain fatty acids. No mead acid, the classic essential fatty acid deficiency signal.

Polyunsaturated fats have more double bonds by definition, which makes them more chemically vulnerable to oxidative damage than saturated or monounsaturated fat. 8 · Genetics

Genetics answers a question no other panel can: is this pattern something you built, or something you inherited? The answer changes the entire strategy. Someone with an inherited receptor defect is not going to lifestyle their way to a normal ApoB, and telling them otherwise sets them up to fail and then blame themselves.

◆ What Genetics Is Actually For

Genetics loads the gun. Lifestyle and a personalised protocol decide whether the trigger ever gets pulled.

Read this section as a blueprint, not a verdict. None of these variants is a sentence — not APOE e4, not a high-Lp(a) genotype, not even familial hypercholesterolemia. What they tell you is where your particular system is structurally vulnerable, so the protocol can be built to defend that specific weakness instead of applying a generic one. A person who knows they are an e4 carrier and an efficient absorber, and builds accordingly, can plausibly do better than someone with a “clean” genome and no plan at all. The gene sets the terrain. It does not write the outcome.

Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH)

Mutations in the LDL receptor gene itself, in ApoB (the part the receptor grabs), or gain-of-function mutations in PCSK9. Autosomal dominant, and far more common than most people assume — roughly 1 in 250. These individuals run LDL-C above 190 from childhood regardless of diet.

When to suspect it: LDL-C persistently above 190 despite a genuinely clean lifestyle · a first-degree relative with a heart attack before 55 (men) or 65 (women) · ApoB disproportionately elevated relative to otherwise excellent metabolic markers · tendon xanthomas or corneal arcus before age 45. Here, “why is your body making more cholesterol, what's it responding to?” is the wrong question. It is not an adaptive response to anything — it is a structural clearance defect.

APOE Genotype — The Brain Crossover

Three alleles — e2, e3, e4 — giving six possible genotypes. ApoE clears chylomicron and VLDL remnants via the liver, and separately serves as the primary cholesterol shuttle inside the brain, produced by astrocytes to deliver cholesterol to neurons for membrane repair and synapse formation.

e4 carriers run higher LDL-C on average, absorb dietary cholesterol more efficiently, and respond more strongly to saturated fat — genuinely more diet-responsive. e4 is also the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. The leading explanation ties both to the same isoform being less efficient at its brain cholesterol-delivery job. For an e4 carrier, the lipid conversation and the cognitive conversation are the same conversation.

e2 carriers clear remnants more slowly due to weaker receptor binding — usually showing as lower LDL-C. But in e2/e2 homozygotes combined with additional metabolic stress (diabetes, hypothyroidism, obesity), remnant particles accumulate and risk rises despite a deceptively normal LDL-C. A clean example of why LDL-C alone misleads in both directions.

LPA Gene

A copy-number variant in the kringle IV type 2 domain determines Lp(a) almost entirely — fewer repeats produce a smaller, more efficiently secreted apo(a) and therefore higher circulating Lp(a). Heritability runs 70–90%. This same isoform variability is exactly why mg/dL and nmol/L don't convert cleanly.

PCSK9 — And The Best Natural Experiment In This Field

The LDL receptor grabs ApoB particles, gets pulled into the cell, and is normally recycled back to the surface to work again. PCSK9 binds that receptor and redirects it to the lysosome for destruction instead. More PCSK9 activity means fewer receptors means higher circulating LDL.

A small percentage of people carry loss-of-function PCSK9 variants — their receptors survive, so they clear particles efficiently their whole lives. The size of the benefit tracks the size of the effect: carriers of rare nonsense variants ran roughly 28% lower LDL and about 88% lower coronary risk, while carriers of the far commoner R46L variant ran about 15% lower LDL and roughly 47% lower risk. That is about as close to a natural randomized trial as this field will ever get, and it is why the causal direction is not seriously in dispute. The honest limit: it proves what a lifetime of lower exposure does. It does not fully answer what happens when you lower particles at 55 after decades of higher exposure — a fair question that deserves an honest “less certain.”

Absorption vs. Synthesis — Are You A Hyperabsorber Or A Hyperproducer?

NPC1L1 is the intestinal transporter that pulls cholesterol out of the gut; ABCG5 and ABCG8 pump it back out. Common polymorphisms in these genes create a continuum of absorption efficiency across the whole population — not just the rare disease state.

You can test this directly: campesterol and sitosterol are absorption markers, lathosterol is a synthesis marker. This is the most precision-point move available in the entire lipid workup, because it tells a specific person whether their diet is actually doing anything to their number — instead of handing everyone the same “most cholesterol is made internally” line.

◆ THREE PHENOTYPES, THREE DIFFERENT LEVERS — AND THE RIGHT MARKER FOR EACH

Hyperabsorber (high campesterol and sitosterol, low lathosterol) → your gut is unusually efficient at pulling cholesterol in. Dietary cholesterol genuinely matters more for you than the population average suggests, and fiber, plant sterols and ezetimibe are unusually effective. Statins will underwhelm.

Hyperproducer (low absorption markers, high lathosterol) → your liver is the source. Statins, red yeast rice and berberine target synthesis directly. Diet moves your number less, and telling you to cut eggs is largely wasted advice.

APOE e4 carrier → saturated fat is your lever, and this is the important precision point: sterol markers do not predict this. Saturated fat raises LDL through a different mechanism — it downregulates LDL receptor activity and shifts the hepatic cholesterol pool, rather than increasing intestinal absorption. So sterol markers tell you about your response to dietary cholesterol; APOE genotype and baseline insulin sensitivity tell you about your response to saturated fat. Two different exposures, two different markers. Conflating them is a common and consequential error.

Where this stops: these markers guide which lever to pull on your particle count. They do not support extending the logic to claims about saturated fat and all-cause mortality — that is well beyond what a sterol panel can carry.

◆ The Honest Caveat On All Of It

Most ApoB variation in the general population is not explained by any single gene above. It's polygenic — hundreds of common variants each nudging the number slightly. Polygenic risk scores are beginning to refine risk in people with a worrying family history but no identified single mutation. Worth knowing this exists; not worth building your first decisions around.

♀ Female-Specific Considerations

Menopause is a lipid event, not just a hormone event. Estrogen upregulates LDL receptor expression. When it declines, receptor expression falls and LDL-C and ApoB rise — often substantially, and often over a short window. This is routinely misread as an age or willpower problem when it is a receptor problem. Perimenopause can begin as early as 35.

Lp(a) rises at menopause too, in a marker most people are told never changes. If Lp(a) was measured premenopausally, it is worth one recheck after the transition.

Bio-identical hormone therapy has real, measurable and route-dependent lipid effects. This is one of the most under-discussed levers in a female cardiometabolic workup, so it is worth spelling out properly. Everything below refers to bio-identical forms only — 17β-estradiol, micronised progesterone, and bio-identical testosterone — not to synthetic analogues, which behave differently and sometimes oppositely.

Estradiol — route dominates. Oral 17β-estradiol passes through the liver first, and that first pass is the whole story: it raises triglycerides, raises HDL, lowers LDL, and lowers Lp(a) by roughly 20% — one of very few interventions that moves Lp(a) at all. It also raises CRP, SHBG and several clotting factors. Transdermal estradiol bypasses the liver on first pass: roughly triglyceride-neutral, a smaller HDL rise, still lowers LDL, much less effect on inflammatory and clotting markers, and a lower venous thromboembolism risk — but correspondingly less Lp(a) benefit. So there is a genuine trade-off rather than a right answer: oral does more for Lp(a), transdermal is metabolically and thrombotically cleaner. For a woman with high Lp(a) and otherwise low clotting risk, that trade-off is worth an actual conversation.

Micronised progesterone — and the clearest argument anywhere for bio-identical. Bio-identical micronised progesterone is essentially lipid-neutral and, critically, does not blunt estrogen's HDL benefit. Medroxyprogesterone acetate, the synthetic progestin used in the older trials, substantially does. That difference was demonstrated directly in head-to-head trial data, and it is one of the cleanest, most concrete arguments for bio-identical over synthetic anywhere in medicine — the same estrogen, a different progestogen, and a measurably different lipid outcome. Micronised progesterone is also mildly sedating, so it commonly improves sleep quality, which feeds straight back into Sleep & Light, ranked #3 in this protocol.

Testosterone in women. Low-dose transdermal or cream testosterone is broadly lipid-neutral at physiologic replacement doses. Oral and methylated androgens are a different matter — they lower HDL substantially and should generally be avoided when there is a transdermal option.

Timing matters, and the evidence tier drops here. Initiation within roughly 10 years of menopause, or before age 60, is associated with neutral-to-favourable cardiovascular outcomes; later initiation less so. The lipid effects above are Tier 1 — directly measured and reproducible. The cardiovascular outcome claim is Tier 2 at best, resting on observational data and subgroup analyses rather than a trial designed to answer it. Worth knowing the difference before anyone presents hormone therapy as cardiovascular prevention.

PCOS is a distinct atherogenic phenotype — insulin resistance, high triglycerides, low HDL and small dense LDL, frequently present decades before anyone screens for cardiovascular risk.

Pregnancy history is cardiovascular history. Preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension and preterm delivery are all independently associated with elevated long-term cardiovascular risk. This information is sitting in most women's histories and almost never gets used. And standard risk calculators systematically underestimate risk in women — a normal-looking score is not the reassurance it appears to be.

♂ Male-Specific — Testosterone And The Lipid Picture

Bio-identical testosterone replacement in genuinely hypogonadal men produces a mixed but broadly favourable metabolic picture, with one variable that matters specifically in this protocol and rarely gets discussed in that context.

The favourable side: improved insulin sensitivity, reduced visceral adipose tissue, and increased lean mass — all three of which act directly on the engine driving the atherogenic triad. It can also lower Lp(a) modestly, which is notable given how little else does.

The trade-offs: testosterone modestly lowers HDL, with variable and generally small effects on LDL. Whether the HDL reduction carries real risk is genuinely unsettled — given everything this protocol says about HDL quantity versus HDL function, a fall in HDL alongside falling visceral fat and improving insulin sensitivity is not the same event as a fall in HDL in someone deteriorating metabolically.

The variable most people miss: testosterone raises hematocrit, and rising hematocrit raises blood viscosity. That matters here specifically because this protocol devotes an entire section to flow — wall shear stress, laminar versus disturbed flow, and how flow determines where plaque lands. Thicker blood changes flow characteristics and increases cardiac work. Hematocrit is not an optional monitoring parameter on testosterone therapy; it is arguably the most protocol-relevant one. Therapeutic phlebotomy or dose adjustment resolves it, but only if someone is actually checking.

Route matters here too: oral methylated androgens lower HDL substantially and carry hepatic risk — injectable, transdermal and cream preparations of bio-identical testosterone do not behave the same way. And as with women, physiologic replacement in a genuinely deficient man is a different intervention from supraphysiologic dosing in a man with normal levels, which is a different conversation entirely and not one this protocol addresses.

◆ HOW THIS TREE WORKS
Standard care branches on risk scores — statistical estimates of what someone like you might do. This tree branches on what is actually in your arteries, then uses labs to decide how hard to push. Anatomy first, arithmetic second. Work through the steps in order.
Step 1 · Do you know your Lp(a)?

If no — get it once, before anything else. It is largely genetic, it changes how aggressively you drive every other lever, and most people go their entire lives without it being ordered. Request nmol/L when possible.

If <50 nmol/L (~<20 mg/dL): one meaningful risk multiplier is off the table. Proceed to Step 2.

If 50–125 nmol/L: elevated. Everything else in this protocol matters more for you than for the average person, because you are carrying a fixed baseline burden you cannot negotiate away.

If >125 nmol/L: substantially elevated. This is the profile where imaging moves from optional to strongly advisable regardless of how good the rest of the panel looks, and where an ApoB target of <70 or lower is reasonable. Screening first-degree relatives is worth raising — this is inherited.

Step 2 · What is actually in your arteries?

Every marker discussed so far is a proxy for risk. Imaging is direct evidence. A person with an alarming ApoB and zero plaque is in a completely different conversation than the same labs with substantial plaque — and no risk calculator can tell those two people apart.

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) is inexpensive, widely available, low radiation, and often available for cash without a referral. It is the reasonable entry point.

Coronary CT angiography with AI-enabled plaque quantification goes considerably further — it identifies and measures soft, non-calcified plaque, which calcium scoring cannot see at all and which is the more rupture-prone kind. See the Testing Arsenal and Cutting-Edge tabs.

◆ THE FALLACY OF THE POWER OF ZERO

A calcium score of zero does not mean clean arteries. CT angiography still finds atherosclerosis in a meaningful share of people whose calcium score is zero. How large a share depends heavily on how the scan is read: conventional visual reading puts it around 10–15%, while AI-based quantification, which detects far smaller plaque volumes, finds it in a considerably higher proportion. And because people with a calcium score of zero make up such a large slice of everyone screened, a meaningful fraction of all cardiac events still arises from that supposedly reassured group — even though each individual's risk is genuinely lower. Calcified plaque is, in a real sense, plaque that has already been stabilised. The dangerous material is the soft plaque that hasn't calcified yet — and a calcium scan is blind to it.

Serial imaging shows that plaque is not a one-way street. That distinction is invisible to a calcium score.

◆ How This Tab Is Written

Neither “statins are poison” nor “everyone over 50 should take one.”

Both of those positions are marketing, just aimed at different audiences. Statins

◆ THE HONEST CASE FOR

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis. The liver responds by upregulating LDL receptors, pulling ApoB particles out of circulation. The outcome evidence in secondary prevention — people who already have established disease — is robust and consistent across decades of trials. They also appear to stabilise plaque and reduce inflammation somewhat independently of lipid lowering. If you have Stage 2–3 plaque, or familial hypercholesterolemia, the argument for is strong and dismissing it on ideological grounds is not serving you.

◆ THE HONEST CASE AGAINST — OR AT LEAST FOR CAUTION

In primary prevention — no established disease — absolute risk reductions are considerably more modest than relative risk headlines suggest, and the number needed to treat over five years is high. Muscle symptoms are common in practice even though blinded trials suggest much of it is nocebo. Statins deplete CoQ10 by blocking the same mevalonate pathway that produces it — that mechanism is certain, though trials of CoQ10 for statin myopathy are genuinely mixed.

And the tension this protocol will not paper over: statins modestly increase the risk of new-onset type 2 diabetes — roughly a 9–12% relative increase, concentrated almost entirely in people who already have metabolic risk factors. In a protocol that treats insulin resistance as the engine of atherogenic dyslipidemia, that matters. It does not mean statins are net harmful in someone with real plaque. It does mean the person most likely to be offered one is often the person most vulnerable to that side effect — and that fixing the metabolic terrain underneath should not be deferred just because a number improved.

Ezetimibe

What it does: blocks the NPC1L1 transporter in the small intestine, reducing cholesterol absorption — the exact same target that plant sterols compete at. Typically lowers LDL-C 15–20% alone, more when added to a statin.

For: very well tolerated, no muscle effects, and it is the phenotype-matched drug for a confirmed hyperabsorber — which is exactly why the sterol markers in the Labs tab are worth running. What it does: inhibits ATP-citrate lyase, one step upstream of HMG-CoA reductase. Its clever feature is that it requires an activating enzyme found in liver but not in skeletal muscle — so it lowers cholesterol without the muscle exposure that drives statin intolerance.

For: the most rational option for genuine statin intolerance, with outcome trial support in that population. Against: newer and expensive; raises uric acid and can precipitate gout; small increase in tendon rupture risk.

Against: expensive, injectable, often requires insurance battles, and generally reserved for familial hypercholesterolemia, established disease, or genuine statin intolerance. Long-term data is shorter than for statins simply because they are newer.

◆ WHY RETATRUTIDE APPEARS TO BE THE STRONGER AGENT — AND WHERE IT FALLS SHORT

The mechanism is the reason. Semaglutide activates one receptor. Tirzepatide adds GIP, which improves insulin sensitivity and appears to reduce the nausea burden. Retatrutide adds a third: glucagon receptor agonism. That is the meaningful addition, because glucagon signalling increases energy expenditure and drives hepatic fat mobilisation directly. So instead of working almost entirely by suppressing intake, retatrutide also raises the other side of the equation — and it attacks liver fat, which sits upstream of the VLDL overproduction that drives the entire atherogenic triad in this protocol.

In phase 2 trials it produced roughly 24% body weight reduction at 48 weeks — a magnitude previously seen only with bariatric surgery — and near-complete resolution of hepatic steatosis in a majority of participants with fatty liver.

The shortcomings, stated plainly: it is not approved and cannot be legitimately obtained through normal channels. It has no cardiovascular outcome data at all — and this protocol has spent an entire tab explaining why moving numbers is not the same as preventing events. Gastrointestinal side effects rise with dose. A transient heart rate increase has been observed. And the same concern that applies to the whole class applies more here: rapid weight loss carries meaningful loss of lean mass unless protein intake and resistance training are deliberately protected. Which is exactly why DEXA body composition, not the bathroom scale, is the right way to monitor anyone on these agents.

Independently, CoQ10 sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane shuttling electrons in the respiratory chain — and cardiac muscle, being the most metabolically demanding tissue you own, carries the highest concentration in the body.

Ubiquinone vs. ubiquinol — the actual difference. These are the same molecule in two oxidation states. Ubiquinone is the oxidised form: cheaper, more chemically stable on the shelf, and the form used in the majority of published clinical trials. Ubiquinol is the reduced, electron-carrying form: more expensive, less shelf-stable, and marketed as better absorbed. Your body interconverts the two continuously, and plasma CoQ10 exists predominantly as ubiquinol regardless of which one you swallowed.

The common claim that anyone over 40 needs ubiquinol because conversion declines with age gets repeated constantly and has thin human evidence behind it. Head-to-head absorption studies are mixed once formulation is controlled for. Practical position: start with ubiquinone if cost matters and your levels test adequate — it is considerably cheaper and it works. Move to ubiquinol if levels stay low despite supplementation, if you have fat malabsorption or gut compromise, or if you are on a high statin dose. Let testing decide, not a birthday.

Absorption is where the real gains are — and it is mostly about fat. CoQ10 is strongly fat-soluble and absorption is poor without dietary fat present. Always take it with a meal containing real fat — eggs, avocado, olive oil, butter, fatty fish. Taken with black coffee or on an empty stomach, much of the dose is wasted regardless of which form you bought. Choose an oil-based softgel or a solubilised formulation over a dry powder capsule, and split doses above 200 mg, because absorption saturates. Those three choices matter more than the ubiquinone-versus-ubiquinol argument.

DEXA — Read Far More Than Body Fat Percentage

Most people get a DEXA, look at one number, and miss almost everything the scan actually measured. Four readings matter here:

1. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT), specifically. This is the reading that matters most for this protocol and the one most often ignored. Visceral fat is metabolically active tissue that secretes IL-6 and other inflammatory signals directly into the portal circulation, driving hepatic VLDL output, insulin resistance and systemic inflammation. It is the fat that produces the atherogenic triad. Subcutaneous fat behaves very differently. A DEXA separates them; a bathroom scale and a BMI cannot.

2. Distribution, not just total. Where fat sits carries independent information — android (trunk) versus gynoid (hip and thigh) distribution, and the android-to-gynoid ratio. Two people at identical body fat percentage with opposite distribution patterns have meaningfully different metabolic risk. This is also why the classic “normal weight, high visceral fat” phenotype gets missed entirely by weight-based screening.

3. Lean mass percentage and appendicular lean mass. Muscle is your largest glucose disposal organ. More of it means lower circulating insulin, which means less VLDL and fewer small dense particles. Low lean mass with normal weight — sarcopenic obesity — carries worse outcomes than obesity with preserved muscle.

4. Bone mineral density, which comes free with the scan and matters increasingly with age — and connects back to vitamin K2 and where your calcium is being deposited.

Where this becomes essential: anyone using a GLP-1 class medication should be tracked by DEXA, not by the scale. Rapid weight loss carries substantial lean mass loss unless protein intake and resistance training are deliberately protected — and the scale cannot tell you which kind of weight you lost. Losing muscle while lowering ApoB is not a win.

AI-Enabled Plaque Quantification

Advanced Imaging

Coronary CT angiography analysed by AI-enabled quantification software (i.e. Cleerly and comparable platforms) identifies, characterises and measures every form of plaque vessel by vessel — not just the calcified fraction a calcium score can see. In validation work, AI-quantified plaque outperformed calcium scoring, stenosis severity and conventional risk scores for predicting long-term events.

Why composition matters more than calcium. Low-density non-calcified plaque (red) carries the highest risk of MACE — major adverse cardiovascular events, meaning heart attack, stroke, or cardiovascular death; calcified plaque (blue) is the most stable. A calcium score sees only the blue — the safest material — and is blind to the red. This is the entire reason a calcium score of zero cannot rule out high-risk disease. ◆ WHAT MAKES THIS GENUINELY DIFFERENT

It sees soft plaque. CT angiography still finds atherosclerosis in people with a calcium score of zero — around 10–15% on conventional reading, and a considerably higher proportion when AI quantification is used, because it detects far smaller plaque volumes. And since calcium-score-zero patients make up a large share of everyone screened, a meaningful fraction of all cardiac events still comes from that group, even though each person's individual risk is genuinely lower.

It measures change over time. Serial scans show that treatment can convert high-risk low-density plaque into stable calcified plaque — total volume may barely move while composition transforms. That is a fundamentally more useful endpoint than a single score, and it turns a scary word like “plaque” into something you can actually track and influence.

The honest limitations: more expensive than a calcium score, requires iodinated contrast (so kidney function and allergy matter), delivers more radiation than a calcium scan, is not universally covered by insurance, and requires a facility with the right scanner and software. It is not the right first test for everyone — it is the right test when the answer will change what you do.

Peptide Therapy

Off-Label · Physician Partnership Required

Peptides are short chains of amino acids that act as signalling molecules. The category is often discussed as though it were one thing. It isn't — and the most useful distinction is how close a given peptide is to something your body already makes. That framing is a clinical heuristic, not a regulatory category, and the boundaries are genuinely blurry.

CATEGORY 1 — BIO-IDENTICAL

Sequences identical to peptides the human body already produces. You are supplementing a signal you already make, not introducing a novel molecule.

MOTS-c — a mitochondrial-derived peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA. Improves insulin sensitivity and metabolic flexibility, and activates AMPK. The most directly relevant peptide to this protocol's metabolic pillar.

Humanin — also mitochondrially encoded; cytoprotective and anti-apoptotic, with signals in endothelial protection. Levels decline with age.

GHK-Cu — a copper-binding tripeptide present in human plasma that falls sharply with age. Supports tissue remodelling, angiogenesis and antioxidant defence.

Thymosin β4 (full-length) — a naturally occurring actin-binding protein with real cardiac repair and angiogenesis data in preclinical models.

Sermorelin — the first 29 amino acids of human growth hormone releasing hormone, which is the biologically active portion. It prompts your own pituitary to release growth hormone in natural pulses rather than replacing it — preserving feedback regulation. The most physiologic option in the growth hormone category.

Fragments of natural peptides, or synthetic molecules designed to mimic a natural ligand. Closely related to human biology but not an exact copy of anything you make.

Aha Moment 1 · Systems Thinking
“You've spent your whole life worrying about what's going into your arteries. When did anyone last ask whether the drain works?”
Cholesterol does not leave the arterial wall through the bloodstream. It leaves through the lymphatic system — outward, through vessels wrapped around the artery. Researchers blocked lymphatic drainage two different ways, surgically and genetically, then tracked cholesterol clearance. It collapsed in both models, including from the aortic wall — and it failed even at sites where the blood vessels themselves were leaky enough that cholesterol could in theory have escaped that way instead. And here is the intriguing implication: this may be part of why raising HDL pharmacologically has never delivered the benefit people expected. More trucks sent to a loading dock with no road out. Hold that loosely — it is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated link, and there are competing explanations, including off-target drug effects and the fact that HDL function matters more than HDL quantity. One agent in that class, anacetrapib, did modestly reduce events — though the benefit tracked its ApoB lowering rather than the HDL rise. Lymph has no central pump — the vessels contract rhythmically on their own, and that intrinsic pumping is powerfully assisted by muscle contraction and by the pressure swings of deep breathing. Which means Movement and Air aren't general wellness advice in this protocol. They are a direct assist to the system clearing your arteries.

Aha Moment 2 · Systems Thinking
“If cholesterol is dissolved evenly through your blood — the same blood reaching every inch of 60,000 miles of vessel — why does plaque appear in the same specific handful of spots in nearly everyone?”
Because it isn't about concentration. It's about flow. Plaque clusters at branch points, at bifurcations and along the inner curvature of bends — exactly where flow separates, recirculates and reverses with each beat. Researchers mapped wall shear stress in a model of the human carotid bifurcation, then compared it against actual plaque thickness in cadaver carotids at matching points. Plaque formed precisely where shear was low and oscillating. Endothelial cells read flow: steady laminar flow switches on a protective gene program with more nitric oxide and less inflammation; disturbed flow switches on the opposite. The wall is pre-inflamed and pre-permeable before a single particle arrives. Particles determine how much gets deposited. Flow determines where the wall was willing to let them in.

Aha Moment 3 · Timeline & Causality
“What if a meaningful share of the cholesterol inside your plaque never arrived from your bloodstream at all?”
Growing plaque recruits its own blood supply — the vasa vasorum, the artery's own tiny vessels — and those fragile new vessels bleed into the plaque. Red blood cell membranes are extremely cholesterol-rich, so intraplaque hemorrhage dumps free cholesterol directly into the plaque core. Plaque grows partly from the outside in. This is mainstream cardiology, and it partially vindicates the camp that has long argued bleeding and clotting matter more than the standard lipid story allows.

Aha Moment 4 · Organism & Microbiology
“Could your mouthwash be raising your blood pressure?”
Yes, measurably. Dietary nitrate from leafy greens and beets is reduced to nitrite by bacteria living on your tongue, and then to nitric oxide — a pathway entirely separate from the eNOS enzyme route. Wipe out those bacteria with antiseptic mouthwash and the pathway shuts down; blood pressure rises within days and returns when the rinsing stops. Your cardiovascular system is outsourcing a critical step to organisms that aren't you — and a product marketed for cleanliness disrupts it.

Aha Moment 5 · Systems Thinking
“Everyone argues about whether heart disease is a cholesterol problem or an inflammation problem. What if the cholesterol is the inflammation?”
Cholesterol accumulating in plaque forms actual crystals — and those crystals physically activate the NLRP3 inflammasome inside macrophages, releasing IL-1β. That is not a metaphor; it is a physical trigger. And it is the exact pathway that was later drugged successfully: an antibody against IL-1β that does nothing whatsoever to lipids reduced recurrent cardiac events. It is also why low-dose colchicine, an old inexpensive gout drug that inhibits the same inflammasome, has shown cardiovascular benefit. The lipid camp and the inflammation camp have been arguing about two ends of one mechanism.

Aha Moment 6 · Conventional Medicine Blind Spot
“Your calcium score came back zero and you were told your arteries are clean. How confident should you be?”
Less confident than you were told. CT angiography still finds plaque in people whose calcium score is zero — roughly 10 to 15% on conventional reading, and a considerably higher share when AI quantification is used, because it picks up far smaller volumes. And because calcium-score-zero patients are such a large slice of everyone screened, a meaningful fraction of all cardiac events still comes out of that group, even though any one person's risk there is genuinely lower. The reason is almost counter-intuitive: calcification is largely how the body stabilises plaque. A calcium scan sees the safest material and is blind to the soft, low-density plaque that is far more likely to rupture. Cardiology literature has bluntly named this ‘the fallacy of the power of zero.’

Aha Moment 7 · Timeline & Causality
“A small number of people were born with a natural version of one of our most expensive cholesterol drugs. What happened to them?”
They carry a loss-of-function mutation in PCSK9 — the protein that normally destroys LDL receptors. Their receptors survive, so they clear particles efficiently their entire lives and run lower LDL. And the benefit scales with the effect: carriers of rare nonsense variants ran about 28% lower LDL and roughly 88% lower coronary risk; carriers of the much commoner R46L variant ran about 15% lower LDL and roughly 47% lower risk. That is as close to a randomized trial as nature provides, and it is why the causal direction is not seriously disputed even by thoughtful skeptics. It is also the origin story of the PCSK9 inhibitor drug class — a rare case of human genetics translating almost directly into a medicine. The honest limit worth adding: it proves what a lifetime of lower exposure does. It doesn't fully answer what lowering particles at 55 accomplishes after decades of higher exposure.

Aha Moment 9 · Brain–Body Connection
“Your brain holds roughly a quarter of all the cholesterol in your body. How much of it comes from your blood?”
Essentially none. The delivery vehicle is ApoE, which is precisely why the APOE e4 variant sits at the intersection of cardiovascular and cognitive risk. So a person taking a drug to lower serum cholesterol is not draining their brain — the two pools are largely separate. And at the same time, the brain is utterly dependent on cholesterol it makes itself, which is part of why membrane cholesterol influences serotonin receptor function and why serum levels that run too low associate with depressed mood.

Aha Moment 10 · Conventional Medicine Blind Spot
“A muscle the size of your fist is supposed to push blood through 60,000 miles of vessel. Does that actually add up?”
It does — but only once you stop picturing a single long hose. The vascular tree is a massively parallel network, and in a parallel circuit adding more paths lowers total resistance. Nearly all the pressure drop happens across the arterioles; by the capillary bed, total cross-sectional area has expanded roughly a thousandfold and very little driving pressure is needed. The heart also gets help it rarely gets credit for: elastic arteries stretch during systole and recoil during diastole, smoothing flow, and venous return is substantially assisted by the calf muscle pump and by the negative pressure in your chest when you inhale. And the ejection itself is not a simple push — blood moves through arteries in a spiral, a pattern that resists separation at bends and branches and maintains higher wall shear. Which means the heart's twisting motion may be athero-protective by design.

Aha Moment 11 · Conventional Medicine Blind Spot
“Your angiogram was clean and your stress test was normal. Why do most heart attacks come from arteries that looked fine?”
Because arteries hide plaque by growing outward. As plaque accumulates, the vessel remodels and enlarges to preserve the opening — so the lumen looks normal on an angiogram until plaque occupies roughly 40% of the cross-sectional area. Everything before that point is invisible to a test that only measures narrowing. This is why the majority of heart attacks arise from lesions that were not obstructive, and why plaque volume and composition are better predictors than percent stenosis. A stress test asks whether flow is currently limited. It does not ask whether there is something in the wall waiting to rupture.
