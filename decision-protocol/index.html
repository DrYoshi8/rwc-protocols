◆ DOES THIS RESONATE?

You might be living this pattern if…

  • Someone looked at one number, reached for a prescription pad, and the visit was over before you understood what the number meant.
  • You have a folder of results going back years and no idea which of them actually matter.
  • Every practitioner hands you a different list of supplements, and the lists do not agree.
  • A result was flagged, you were told to repeat it in a year, and nobody asked what might have caused it.
  • You have been told a value is “normal for your age” and something in you did not accept that as an answer.
  • You are doing a great many things and cannot tell which of them is working.
◆ The Assumption Worth Questioning

“If a number is abnormal, it needs treating.”

A result is not an observation of your body. It is the output of a procedure — a method, applied under particular conditions, on a particular morning, judged against a range somebody chose. Before any of it means anything, you have to know how it was made and what is driving it. Treating a number without that is treating a rumour. What would have to be true for this result to be telling you something other than what it appears to say?

What this protocol is

Every other RoVive protocol answers what — what the markers mean, what the options are, what to take. This one answers how to decide. It is a method, not a topic.

That means it is deliberately domain-neutral. The structure below works the same whether the question in front of you is a lipid panel, a thyroid result, a gut test, a hormone panel, a bone density scan or an imaging report. The examples have to come from somewhere, so several are cardiovascular — but nothing in the reasoning depends on that, and the Worked Examples tab deliberately includes thyroid and gut cases to show the same five questions doing the same work in different territory.

What it is not

It does not restate doses. Where a decision leads to a specific agent, this protocol points you to the one that carries the detail — the Lipid & Vascular protocol, the Fats & Fatty Acids protocol, and the rest of the library. Duplicating dosing across documents is how two documents end up disagreeing within a year.

It is not a substitute for judgment, and it is not a substitute for a physician. It is a way of making your own reasoning visible, so that you can tell the difference between a decision and a guess — in yourself, and in the people advising you.

The Core Reframe — The Treatment Triangle

Three layers. You build from the bottom. The layer almost everyone reaches for first is the smallest one at the top.

The Treatment Triangle. TERRAIN is what is driving the number — and it maps directly onto the Great 8 Pillars, which is why they are the second tab of this protocol rather than an afterthought. TRAJECTORY asks whether anything is actually happening: imaging, a controlled repeat, a rate of change over time. TREATMENT is the smallest band because it is the last decision, not the first. The red arrow is where a twelve-minute appointment enters the diagram; the green arrow is the order this protocol argues for.
◆ The Inversion

Conventional care enters at the apex and usually never descends. A value crosses a threshold and a prescription follows. That is not malice and it is not incompetence — it is a short appointment and a guideline written for populations rather than for you. The whole of this protocol is an argument for entering at the base instead.

How to read the evidence tiers

Every claim, marker and intervention in the RoVive library carries a tier badge where it is first covered in detail. The tier describes how much and what kind of evidence exists — not how confident you should feel about your own body.

The tier system, widest at the bottom because that is where most of biology currently sits. A protocol containing only Tier 1 material would be short, safe and far less useful. Most real clinical judgment happens in Tier 2. Tier 3 is where the interesting questions live — and naming something as unproven while still including it is what makes the rest of the document trustworthy.

Why the bottom tier still belongs here. Excluding everything unproven would mean excluding most of what a thoughtful clinician actually thinks about. The honest move is not to hide Tier 3 material but to label it, so you can tell the difference between something you should act on and something worth watching. A protocol that never says “we do not know” is not being careful with you.

Nothing in biology is monotonic

This protocol spends much of its length arguing against over-treating. It is worth stating the opposite risk just as plainly: under-treating is also a decision, and it also has consequences. Declining a genuinely indicated intervention because a document encouraged scepticism is the same error in the other direction. The goal is not less medicine. It is medicine that has earned its place — and sometimes, having asked the questions honestly, the answer is yes, start it, start it now.

◆ THE GREAT 8 PILLARS — THE TERRAIN LAYER
In this protocol the pillars are not a wellness checklist appended to the end. They are the terrain — the base of the Treatment Triangle, and the answer to “what is driving this number?” almost every time anyone bothers to look.
◆ HOW TO READ THIS LIST
All 8 pillars matter — always. Because this is a method rather than a condition, the ranking here is unusual: it is ordered by how often a pillar turns out to be the driver that nobody looked at, not by importance. For any specific condition the order changes, and the relevant protocol will rank them for that condition. Your weakest pillar is often your biggest opportunity regardless of where it sits here.
#1
Sleep & Light

The most commonly missed upstream driver in all of medicine, and the one most likely to be generating several apparently separate problems at once. Disordered or insufficient sleep raises blood pressure, degrades glucose handling, raises inflammatory markers, suppresses testosterone, impairs memory consolidation and slows processing speed. A person with four abnormal results and untreated sleep-disordered breathing does not have four problems.

◆ HOW IT SHOWS UP IN A DECISION
Before accepting any list of separate diagnoses, ask whether one sleep problem could produce all of them. If it could, the sleep study is the highest-yield test on the list — and the other decisions can reasonably wait for it.

The glymphatic system clears metabolic waste from the brain predominantly during slow-wave sleep, and that clearance falls sharply when deep sleep is fragmented. Growth hormone release is pulsatile and concentrated in early-night slow-wave sleep. Circadian misalignment independently impairs insulin sensitivity in controlled laboratory studies, in people with entirely normal sleep duration.

The decision-relevant point is not that sleep is important — everyone agrees it is important. It is that sleep-disordered breathing is common, under-diagnosed, and produces a constellation of findings that look like independent diseases. Hypertension, proteinuria, cognitive complaints, elevated inflammatory markers and low testosterone can all be downstream of it.

When several findings appear at once, the discipline is to ask what single upstream process could generate all of them before treating each one separately. Sleep is the first place to look, because it is common, testable, treatable, and routinely skipped.

Light is inseparable from this. Morning outdoor light exposure entrains the circadian system more powerfully than any supplement; evening blue light delays melatonin onset. Both are free, and both are frequently the actual intervention when someone asks which sleep supplement to take.

#2
Food

Food is the terrain input with the largest surface area and the most contested evidence, which makes it the pillar most likely to be argued about and least likely to be measured. The decision-relevant question is rarely “is this food good or bad” but “what is this person actually eating, how do we know, and what would change if it changed?”

◆ HOW IT SHOWS UP IN A DECISION
Before attributing a marker to diet, establish that the dietary pattern was actually assessed rather than assumed. A great deal of nutritional advice is given to people whose diet was never asked about in any detail.

The recurring failure in nutritional decision-making is treating diet as a fixed belief rather than a testable variable. Three disciplines fix most of it.

Establish the baseline before changing it. A marker attributed to diet, in someone whose intake was never assessed, is an attribution rather than a finding. Photographic food records over several ordinary days outperform recall dramatically.

Change one thing, and define the readout first. Deciding in advance which marker should move, by how much, and over what interval, is what separates an experiment from a hope. If nothing was specified in advance, any result can be read as success.

Match the marker to the exposure. Different markers reflect different time windows. Some reflect the previous morning; others integrate over months. Choosing a marker that moves faster than the intervention, or slower than the follow-up interval, produces an uninterpretable result no matter how good the intervention was.

The full dietary reasoning for any given condition belongs in that condition’s protocol. What belongs here is the decision structure: assess, change one thing, pre-specify the readout, and choose a marker whose time course matches the question.

#3
Movement

Movement is the pillar with the strongest outcome evidence and the weakest adherence, which makes it a decision problem more than an information problem. Almost nobody needs to be told that exercise helps. What they need is a version that survives their actual life.

◆ HOW IT SHOWS UP IN A DECISION
When designing any movement recommendation, ask what will still happen during a bad week, a travel week or an injury. A plan that only works under ideal conditions is a plan that will stop.

Zone 2 cardiovascular work (e.g., a brisk walk where you can hold a full conversation, easy cycling, light swimming, a conversational-pace jog) and progressive resistance training are foundational, and the evidence for both is strong.

But the decision-relevant failure mode is not choosing the wrong modality. It is the all-or-nothing pattern: an ambitious programme, three good weeks, an injury or a disruption, and then nothing for eight months. This pattern is extremely common and it is predictable from someone’s history.

Where that history exists, the correct move is often to deliberately prescribe something smaller than what is optimal — something that survives travel, illness and a bad week. Ten bodyweight squats after each meal is not an optimal glycaemic intervention. It is a real one, and the difference matters far more than the gap between it and something better that will not happen.

The principle generalises well beyond movement: the best version of any intervention is the one that will actually be done. When a plan and a life are in conflict, the plan loses, and the plan was the thing you were able to change.

#4
Spirit & Purpose

Whether that is God, your higher self, a deep belief in yourself, or simply what gets you out of bed before the alarm — this is your animating force. In decision terms it determines whether any plan is sustained, and it shapes what a person is actually optimising for, which is not always what a guideline assumes.

Two people with identical results can reasonably make opposite decisions, and neither is wrong. One is optimising for maximum years; another for independence, or capacity for specific work, or avoiding a particular way of declining that they watched someone else endure.

A protocol that never asks what someone is optimising for will keep producing technically correct recommendations that get quietly abandoned. Ask what the person is actually trying to protect. The answer changes which trade-offs are acceptable — how much monitoring burden is tolerable, how much medication, how much dietary restriction, how much uncertainty.

There is also a measurable dimension. Sense of purpose has been associated with lower all-cause mortality in prospective cohorts, with effect sizes comparable to several interventions that receive vastly more clinical attention. The mechanisms proposed include health behaviour, stress physiology and inflammatory signalling. Tier 2

#5
Community & Connection

The most underestimated pillar, and one of the few terrain inputs that can be observed without any test at all. Social isolation carries mortality associations comparable to established cardiovascular risk factors, and unlike most of them it is rarely recorded, rarely asked about, and almost never entered into a treatment decision.

The decision-relevant observation is that isolation does two things at once: it acts as a direct physiological stressor, and it degrades every other pillar. Isolated people sleep worse, eat worse, move less and take medication less consistently. A plan built for someone who is alone and unsupported has to be simpler than a plan built for someone with help.

There are also population observations that are difficult to explain any other way — communities whose cardiovascular outcomes were substantially better than their measured risk factors predicted, and whose advantage faded as their social structure changed rather than as their diet did. Those observations have real methodological limitations and cannot carry a causal claim on their own. Tier 2

Practical consequence: ask who else is in the room. The answer changes what plan is realistic, and it is free to obtain.

#6
Water

Hydration status silently changes a surprising number of measured values by concentrating or diluting what is measured per unit volume. A person who arrives dehydrated produces a different panel from the same person hydrated, with no change in underlying physiology.

This pillar earns its place in a decision protocol for a specific and unglamorous reason: hydration status is one of the commonest reasons a result is wrong without anyone being at fault. Concentration effects touch creatinine, haemoglobin, haematocrit, albumin and lipid values among others.

It compounds with other pre-analytical factors. Someone who trained hard, used a sauna and arrived under-hydrated can produce several simultaneously abnormal values that all normalise on a controlled repeat. This is covered in detail in The Five Questions → Question 3.

Beyond the analytical point, adequate mineralised water intake supports plasma volume, thermoregulation and renal clearance. The quality conversation — filtration, mineral content, structure — is real but belongs in the protocols where it carries specific weight.

#7
Air

Breathing pattern is a directly modifiable input to autonomic state, and air quality is an exposure that is almost never measured in people who spend most of their lives indoors. Both are terrain; neither appears on any standard panel.

Breath as autonomic lever. Slow nasal breathing with an extended exhale raises vagal tone and heart rate variability within minutes. Nasal breathing generates nitric oxide in the paranasal sinuses, which contributes to airway and vascular tone. These effects are measurable and immediate, which makes breathwork one of the few terrain interventions with a same-session readout.

Air quality as exposure. Indoor air is where most people spend most of their time, and it is essentially never assessed. Combustion sources, mould, volatile organic compounds and particulate matter are real exposures with real physiological consequences, and they can produce findings that look idiopathic.

When inflammatory markers are elevated with no obvious source, exposure history belongs in the differential alongside everything else — including the building the person lives in.

#8
Electrical

Light exposure, grounding, electromagnetic environment and mitochondrial charge. This pillar carries the widest range of evidence quality of any of the eight, from well-established circadian photobiology to claims that remain speculative — which makes honest tiering more important here than anywhere else.

Well established. Light is the dominant circadian entrainment signal, acting through melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells. Timing, intensity and spectrum all matter. Morning outdoor light and evening dimming are among the highest-leverage, lowest-cost interventions available. Tier 1

Reasonable mechanism, developing evidence. Red and near-infrared photobiomodulation has plausible mechanisms involving cytochrome c oxidase and mitochondrial function, with a growing but heterogeneous clinical literature. Effects appear real in several indications; optimal dosing parameters remain unsettled. Tier 2

Mechanistic and epidemiological only. Grounding, structured water and non-ionising electromagnetic field exposure at everyday intensities have proposed mechanisms and some supportive observation, but intervention evidence is limited or absent. Tier 3

This pillar is a useful test of whether a protocol is being honest with you. Including Tier 3 material is legitimate. Presenting it at Tier 1 confidence is not.

Using the pillars as a diagnostic move

When a result is abnormal and nobody can say why, walk the eight in order and ask a single question of each: could this pillar be producing this finding, and has anyone actually checked?

The value is not that you will always find the answer. It is that the walk surfaces the pillars that were assumed rather than assessed — and in practice the driver is usually sitting in one of those.

◆ Before You Treat A Number

Five questions, in order. They work on any result, in any domain, from any laboratory.

The first three ask whether the number is real. The last two ask whether it matters. Most clinical conversations begin at question four without ever asking the first three — which is how a value that was never trustworthy ends up driving a decision that lasts for years.

1

What is this measuring — and in what units?

Two results with the same name are not necessarily the same quantity, and two numbers in different units are not always convertible.

Most unit conversions are harmless arithmetic. Glucose in mmol/L is mg/dL divided by 18. Vitamin D in nmol/L is ng/mL multiplied by 2.5. Both convert exactly, in both directions, for everyone.

Some do not convert at all. Where a measurement reports mass and another reports number of particles, the relationship between them depends on how big the individual particles happen to be in that person — which varies. A single conversion factor is then not merely imprecise; it is systematically wrong in a direction that depends on the individual.

The same problem appears in other guises across medicine:

  • Different assays for the same analyte that are not standardised against one another, so results from two laboratories cannot be compared even in identical units.
  • Total versus free versus bioavailable fractions of a hormone — three different questions reported under similar names, with different reference ranges and different clinical meanings.
  • Activity versus concentration — how much of an enzyme is present is not the same question as how much work it is doing.
  • Antibody titres expressed on scales that differ by laboratory, where a fourfold change may be meaningful and a twofold change may be noise.
◆ WHAT TO DO
Before comparing any two results, confirm they are the same quantity, in the same units, from the same assay. When they are not, the fix is to re-order in the preferred units rather than to convert the number you already have.
2

Was it measured, or was it calculated?

Several of the most-quoted numbers in medicine are never measured at all. They are arithmetic performed on other numbers — which means they inherit every error those numbers carry, and they have conditions under which the arithmetic stops working.

When an equation was never valid at the starting value, comparing before and after is comparing an artefact to a measurement.

◆ WHAT TO DO
For any value driving a decision, ask whether it was measured or derived, and if derived, whether the formula was valid at your values. Where it matters, ask for the direct measurement — it usually exists and is usually simply not ordered by default.
3

◆ WHAT TO DO
For any abnormal result that is about to change a decision, reconstruct the 48 hours before the draw before accepting it. If any of the above applies, the right next step is a controlled repeat — not a diagnosis, and not dismissal either.
4

What is driving it — and has anyone looked?

This is the terrain question, and it is the one most often skipped entirely. A result tells you that something is happening. It does not carry its own cause.

Two people can arrive at an identical value by completely different routes, and the route determines what will actually help. Treating the number without knowing the route means selecting an intervention at random from the list of things that move that number.

The structured version of this question is to walk the Great 8 Pillars and ask of each: could this be producing this finding, and has anyone checked? The value of the walk is that it separates the pillars that were assessed from the pillars that were assumed. In practice the driver is usually sitting among the assumed ones.

◆ THE UPSTREAM TEST

When several findings appear together, ask whether one upstream process could generate all of them. If it could, that process becomes the highest-yield thing to investigate — and the downstream decisions can often reasonably wait for the answer. Four findings do not necessarily mean four problems.

◆ WHAT TO DO
Ask directly: what is driving this, and what was actually looked at to find out? If the honest answer is that nobody looked, that is the next step — not a prescription.
5

Is it actually doing anything?

This is the trajectory question. A risk factor is a statement about probability across a population. It is not a statement about what is happening inside you.

Three kinds of evidence answer it, and they differ enormously in directness:

  • Direct visualisation. Imaging that shows the tissue or structure in question. The most direct evidence available, and usually the most expensive and least often ordered.
  • Functional measurement. A test of what the system can actually do, rather than what a marker implies it might. Capacity testing, provocative testing, performance measures.
  • Rate of change. The same measurement, under the same conditions, over time. Frequently more informative than any single value — and available to anyone with two comparable results.

A marker is a proxy. Proxies are useful precisely because they are cheap and repeatable, and they are limited precisely because they are proxies. When a proxy and a direct measurement disagree, the direct measurement usually wins — and the disagreement is itself one of the most informative findings available.

When does looking earn its cost?

Direct assessment is not always worth it. It earns its place when:

  • The result would change the decision — in either direction. If you would do the same thing regardless, the test is expensive reassurance.
  • The markers and the clinical picture disagree, and the disagreement is unresolved.
  • A long-term commitment is being contemplated, and the evidence for starting rests entirely on proxies.
  • The person needs to see it to act on it — which is a legitimate clinical reason, not a soft one.

It does not earn its place when the result cannot change the plan, when it will generate incidental findings that trigger cascades of further testing with their own risks, or when it is being ordered because the uncertainty is uncomfortable rather than because the answer is actionable.

◆ WHAT TO DO
Before treating a risk factor long-term, ask what direct evidence exists that it is doing damage in this body — and whether obtaining that evidence would change anything. Sometimes the honest answer is that it would not, and the decision proceeds on the proxy. That is a legitimate answer. It is just not the same as never having asked.
◆ The Rule These Five Questions Exist To Teach

An abnormal result is never safe to ignore — and it is rarely safe to act on the first time you see it.

If questions 4 and 5 have not been asked, the next step is to ask them. That is not delay and it is not scepticism for its own sake. It is the difference between a decision and a guess.

Asking in writing

Requests made verbally in a short appointment are the ones that quietly do not get ordered. A short written list, sent in advance through the portal, survives far better — and creates a record of what was asked.

If a request is declined, asking for the reason to be documented is reasonable, not adversarial. Often there is a good reason and hearing it is useful. Occasionally there is not, and the documentation is what lets you take the question elsewhere.

◆ The Sequence

The sequencing rules

Rule 1 · Establish the number before interpreting it

Questions 1 to 3 come first, always, and they are cheap. Units, measured-versus-calculated, and the 48 hours before the draw can all be answered without ordering anything.

The reason this rule comes first is asymmetry. The cost of checking is minutes. The cost of not checking is a diagnosis that follows someone for the rest of their life — in their chart, in their insurance record, and in how every subsequent clinician reads them.

A controlled repeat is the single most under-used instrument in outpatient medicine. It costs one more draw and it resolves a substantial fraction of abnormal results.

Rule 2 · Look for one cause before treating several effects

When several abnormalities appear at once, the reflex is to address each. The discipline is to ask first whether one upstream process could be producing all of them.

If it could, that investigation outranks every downstream decision, because a positive result changes several of them simultaneously and a negative result costs you only the time it took.

This is where the Great 8 Pillars earn their place in a decision protocol. The walk is quick, and it reliably surfaces the pillars that were assumed rather than assessed.

The counterweight: do not let the search for elegance delay something urgent. A unifying explanation is a hypothesis, not a reason to leave a genuinely dangerous value untreated while you look for it. Urgency overrides elegance.

Rule 3 · Name what would change your mind — before you decide

This is the discipline that separates a plan from a guess, and it is almost never done explicitly.

Any decision — to treat, to wait, or to watch — should come with a stated answer to: what result, symptom or timeframe would make me reverse this? Write it down at the moment of deciding, when you are still honest, rather than later when you are defending the decision.

Two things follow from it. A plan with a named trigger has a built-in review date, so it cannot quietly become permanent by inertia. And a plan without one is not really a plan — it is a preference, and it will be defended like one.

◆ THE TEST
If you cannot name what would change your mind, you have not made a decision. You have taken a position.
Rule 4 · Withholding is a decision, and often the right one

The most important decision in many plans is something deliberately not started. It rarely feels like action, which is exactly why it gets skipped.

Deliberate withholding is appropriate when:

  • A pending investigation could change the choice entirely.
  • Treating the downstream number would obscure the upstream cause, or make it harder to find.
  • Starting two things at once would make it impossible to know which one worked.
  • The person’s circumstances mean the intervention will predictably fail right now, and a later start has a better chance.

Withholding is only legitimate when it is named, time-limited and written down — with the trigger from Rule 3 attached. Withholding without a review date is not a decision; it is drift wearing a decision’s clothes.

And the honest converse: withholding something genuinely indicated, because the uncertainty feels safer than the commitment, is a real harm that does not show up in any record.

Rule 5 · Change one variable, and define the readout first

Starting four things at once means that if something improves you will never know which one did it — and you will carry all four, at cost, indefinitely.

Before starting anything, specify three things: which marker or symptom should move, by how much, and by when. Write them down. A readout defined afterwards will be met by almost any outcome.

Where the pressure to do several things at once is real — and sometimes it is — stage them deliberately, with intervals long enough for each to declare itself, rather than abandoning the principle entirely.

The exception worth naming: where an intervention is genuinely a package whose components are not expected to work alone, treat the package as the single variable rather than pretending otherwise.

Rule 6 · Match the plan to the life, not to the literature

The best version of any intervention is the one that will actually be sustained. A plan that is optimal on paper and incompatible with someone’s work, travel, caring responsibilities or finances is not a better plan — it is a plan that will stop.

Where someone has a documented history of starting ambitiously and stopping, that history is clinical data. It predicts the outcome of the next ambitious plan at least as well as any marker predicts anything.

The correct response is usually to deliberately prescribe something smaller than optimal, and to say plainly that this is what you are doing and why. Naming the pattern out loud — without judgement — is often the most useful thing in the conversation.

A modest intervention that happens beats an excellent one that does not. This is not a compromise on standards. It is what taking outcomes seriously actually looks like.

Three shapes a decision takes

Across domains, the same three patterns recur. Recognising which one you are in tells you what the next move is. These are shapes of reasoning, not conditions — each appears in cardiology, endocrinology, gastroenterology and everywhere else.

Shape 1
The markers are alarming and the direct evidence is reassuring
Proxies suggest significant risk. Direct assessment of the tissue or function in question shows far less than the proxies implied. The two genuinely disagree.
  • What it means: the proxy is doing what proxies do — describing a population average that this person is not sitting at. Something in their terrain is not converting risk into damage at the expected rate.
  • The move: direct evidence generally outranks the proxy. This is the pattern where the right answer is frequently to do less than the numbers demand — and the only reason anyone can know that is that somebody looked.
  • The trap: generalising it. One person’s reassuring direct evidence licenses nothing for anyone else, and does not license ignoring the proxy in future either. It buys information about one body at one time.
Shape 2
Several findings, one plausible upstream cause, and a pending test
Multiple abnormalities appear together. One process could plausibly generate all of them, and a single investigation would confirm or exclude it.
  • What it means: treating each finding separately risks four interventions for one problem, and obscures the cause while doing it.
  • The move: prioritise the upstream test above everything downstream, and deliberately withhold the downstream treatments until it returns — with a named trigger and a date.
  • The trap: letting the pending test become indefinite. A withheld decision needs a deadline, or it becomes a decision never to treat, made by inertia rather than by judgement.
Shape 3
Something improved dramatically, and it was not the thing that mattered
A marker moves impressively. A second marker, measuring the thing you actually care about, has barely moved — or a third has moved in a direction that looks alarming but is an artefact of the first one changing.
  • What it means: the intervention did something real. It did not necessarily do the thing the intervention was for.
  • The move: identify in advance which marker actually represents the outcome you care about, and judge by that one. Where a calculated value moved because its inputs moved, exclude it from the comparison entirely.
  • The trap: the celebration. A dramatic improvement in a secondary marker is genuinely motivating, and it is worth acknowledging — while being clear that the primary question is still open.

Deprescribing — the decision nobody schedules

Every item on a plan was started for a reason, at a moment, under conditions. Any of those may have changed. Almost nothing prompts a review.

Ask periodically, of everything on the list: what was this started for, is that still true, and how would I know if it were not? If nobody can answer the first part, that alone is worth investigating.

Stopping is subject to the same rules as starting — one thing at a time, with a defined readout and a named trigger for restarting. And some things must not be stopped abruptly at all, which is a conversation to have with the prescriber before making any change.

◆ What This Tab Is

This tab contains no doses. That is deliberate.

Doses belong in the protocol for the condition being treated, where they sit next to the mechanism, the evidence tier, the interactions and the cautions. Repeating them here would create a second source that drifts out of agreement with the first. What belongs here is the part no condition protocol covers: how to decide whether to take anything at all, how to tell whether it is working, and when to stop.

The first question is not which one

Almost every supplement conversation starts at “which one should I take?” The prior question is whether anything should be added at all, and it has a real answer that differs by person.

Ask this firstWhy it comes before choosing an agent
Is there a measured deficiency, or an inferred one?Correcting a documented deficiency and supplementing on the theory that more is better are different activities with different odds. The first has a defined endpoint and a way to know you have arrived. The second does not.
Would a terrain change do the same job?Several of the commonest supplement targets respond to sleep, light exposure, movement or food — usually more durably, always more cheaply. A supplement taken instead of the terrain change is a tax paid to avoid the harder thing.
What is the readout, and when?If no marker or symptom can be named that should change, and no interval by which it should change, there will be no way to evaluate this later. It will simply accumulate.
What else is already on the list?Interaction risk rises with the number of items, and so does the chance that two products contain the same ingredient. Long lists are frequently the product of several practitioners who never saw each other’s recommendations.
Will this actually be taken?A regimen requiring four separate timings around meals has a predictable adherence curve. Simplicity is a clinical property, not a convenience.

Absorption often matters more than the form debate

A great deal of energy goes into which branded form of a compound is superior, and comparatively little into whether it is being taken in a way that allows absorption at all. The second usually matters more.

  • Fat-soluble compounds need to be taken with a meal containing fat. Taken on an empty stomach, the form is irrelevant because very little is getting in.
  • Some minerals compete with each other for the same transporters. Taken together, they reduce each other’s uptake — separating them can matter more than which salt was chosen.
  • Some compounds require an empty stomach and are blocked by food, calcium or coffee.
  • Divided dosing outperforms a single large dose for several water-soluble compounds with saturable absorption.
  • Timing relative to the circadian cycle matters for anything affecting alertness or sleep — and the same compound can help or harm depending only on when it is taken.

The specific guidance for any given compound lives in the protocol that covers it. The decision principle is simply: before changing the form, confirm the timing is right.

When “natural” is pharmacologically a drug

Some botanical and over-the-counter products contain compounds that are pharmacologically identical, or nearly identical, to prescription medicines. Where that is true, they carry the same interactions, the same monitoring requirements and the same contraindications — while being sold without any of the accompanying counselling.

Three consequences follow, and they apply regardless of how the product is marketed:

  • Interactions are real interactions. A botanical that induces or inhibits a drug-metabolising enzyme changes the blood level of everything else that uses that pathway.
  • Contraindications carry across. If the pharmaceutical version is contraindicated in pregnancy, or with a particular condition, so is the botanical.
  • Content varies between products and between batches in a way that prescription medicines do not, which makes the effective dose genuinely uncertain.

The practical rule: anything with a real pharmacological effect deserves the same disclosure as a prescription. Tell every prescriber what you are taking, including the things that came from a health food shop. The word “natural” on a label is a marketing decision, not a safety finding.

◆ NARROW THERAPEUTIC WINDOW

A small number of compounds — cardiac glycosides above all — have a narrow margin between an effective dose and a toxic one. For these, no dose is published anywhere in the RoVive library, deliberately. They require physician partnership, monitoring, and an understanding of the interactions that raise blood levels unexpectedly. If you encounter a dose for such a compound online, the presence of the number is itself a warning about the source.

How to tell whether it is working

This is where most supplement regimens fail, and the failure is structural rather than pharmacological. Without a pre-specified readout, nothing can be evaluated, so nothing is ever stopped, and the list only grows.

◆ A GOOD READOUT
  • Named before starting
  • Moves on a timescale you are willing to wait for
  • Measured the same way each time
  • Would be convincing to someone sceptical
  • Has a threshold defined in advance
◆ A POOR READOUT
  • Chosen afterwards to fit the result
  • Moves far slower than the review interval
  • Compared across different assays or conditions
  • So broad that anything counts as improvement
  • Entirely subjective, with no diary and no baseline

Subjective readouts are legitimate — energy, sleep quality, digestive comfort, mood and pain are the things people actually care about, and no marker substitutes for them. They simply need the same discipline: a baseline recorded before starting, a consistent way of rating, and a pre-specified interval. A one-to-ten score kept in a note for two weeks before and after is a genuine experiment. A recollection is not.

The review date

Every item added to a plan should be given a date at which it will be reconsidered. Without one, the default is permanence, and the list becomes an archaeological record of every practitioner ever consulted.

At each review, ask of each item:

  • What was this started for?
  • Is that still the case?
  • Did the readout we specified actually move?
  • If I stopped it, what would I expect to happen — and how would I know?

Anything that fails all four is a candidate for stopping. Stop one at a time, with the same discipline as starting, and leave enough time between changes to attribute what happens.

Where the doses actually live

For specific agents, forms, three-tier dosing, evidence grades, interactions and cautions, go to the protocol for the system in question — the Lipid & Vascular protocol, the Fats & Fatty Acids protocol, and the rest of the RoVive library. Each carries its dosing next to the reasoning that justifies it.

If a recommendation appears in two RoVive protocols and they disagree, the condition-specific protocol is the more recent authority and the discrepancy is worth reporting so it can be reconciled.

◆ READ THIS FIRST

These are illustrations of the method. They are not templates, and they are not advice for anybody.

Every example below is a composite, written to show the five questions doing real work. The numbers are illustrative. No example describes a specific person, and none of them should be pattern-matched onto your own results — two people can present with an identical value and need opposite decisions, which is the entire point of the protocol you have just read.

What to take from them is the shape of the reasoning, not the conclusion. The first three are cardiovascular because that is where the illustrations are clearest. The last two are deliberately somewhere else, to show the method does not belong to any one organ system.

Cardiovascular illustrations

EXAMPLE 1 · SHAPE 1

The markers accuse; the imaging acquits

A woman in her sixties. Total cholesterol and LDL are both well above the reference range, particle count is high, and her inflammatory marker is roughly twenty times the optimal target. Two siblings died of heart attacks younger than she is now. Multiple clinicians have recommended lipid-lowering therapy. She eats a diet high in animal fat and has declined.

 Walking the five questions
Q1Units and assays consistent across draws. Nothing ambiguous.
Q2Particle count measured directly rather than estimated. The cholesterol fractions are partly calculated, which matters less here because the particle count is available.
Q3Draws were routine and unremarkable. However the inflammatory marker was drawn during a period of known environmental exposure at home — relevant, and easily missed.
Q4Terrain is mixed. Metabolically she is well: favourable particle-size pattern, no evidence of insulin resistance, exceptionally high protective-particle protein. The inflammation appears to be driven by an environmental exposure rather than by metabolic disease.
Q5Direct imaging of the arteries shows minimal disease for her age, and none of the unstable type. The proxies and the anatomy genuinely disagree.
◆ THE DECISION
Direct evidence outranks the proxy. The decision is to treat nothing for cholesterol, address the environmental exposure, and re-image on an interval — with a named trigger: any meaningful rise in plaque burden, or a change in her metabolic terrain, reopens the question. Note what made this possible: somebody looked. Without the imaging, the same numbers and the same family history would still be an open argument, and a reasonable clinician would still be recommending treatment.
EXAMPLE 2 · SHAPE 2

Four findings, one plausible upstream cause

A man in his mid-sixties arrives to discuss cholesterol. He also has untreated high blood pressure, protein in his urine, subjective memory complaints, and an inflammatory marker roughly twelve times target. Cognitive testing shows a striking deficit in one memory domain alongside slowed processing speed, with attention and executive function intact. He sleeps poorly and snores heavily.

 Walking the five questions
Q1Standard assays, nothing ambiguous.
Q2His filtration rate is calculated from a single marker — worth noting, though his protein loss is measured directly.
Q3Nothing unusual before the draws. Worth confirming for the renal markers specifically.
Q4Four findings, and one candidate that could plausibly generate all of them: untreated sleep-disordered breathing raises blood pressure, contributes to protein loss through pressure effects, drives inflammation, and produces exactly the cognitive pattern seen — slowed speed with preserved higher-order function.
Q5Body composition shows visceral fat in the worst seventh for his age despite an unremarkable appearance. Vascular imaging is pending.
◆ THE DECISION
The sleep study becomes the highest-yield investigation in the entire workup, because a positive result changes several decisions at once. Blood pressure medication is deliberately withheld pending it — with a named trigger and a date — on the reasoning that treating apnoea may resolve the pressure, and treating the pressure would obscure that. A hormonal abnormality is also deferred, since the same upstream process suppresses it. What is started instead is small and terrain-directed: a deficiency corrected, and a movement intervention chosen specifically because it survives an unusually heavy travel schedule.
EXAMPLE 3 · SHAPE 3

The number that moved was not the number that mattered

A metabolically healthy, well-muscled man with a markedly elevated triglyceride level and a high atherogenic particle count. Over four months he takes up daily running, improves his diet and loses weight. He repeats his labs.

 Walking the five questions
Q1Consistent units throughout. One marker was reported in mass units where molar units would have been more informative — a reorder rather than a conversion.
Q2This is where the example turns. His cholesterol fraction was calculated, and at his original triglyceride level the formula was outside its validity range. The starting value was never a measurement.
Q3The first draw was under ordinary conditions. Before the repeat he was given explicit pre-test instructions — and a muscle-derived marker that had suggested organ impairment normalised completely, having been an artefact of training and supplementation.
Q4Terrain is genuinely good: excellent insulin sensitivity, low visceral fat, high muscle mass. The triglyceride elevation is therefore being driven by something other than metabolic disease — hormonal therapy, nicotine and genetics are the candidates.
Q5Triglycerides fell by roughly two thirds. The marker that counts all atherogenic particles fell by about five per cent. The calculated cholesterol fraction rose sharply — because as triglycerides fell, cholesterol redistributed and the formula became valid.
◆ THE DECISION
Four months of genuine effort produced a spectacular improvement in one marker and almost none in the one that represents the actual risk. Judged on the calculated fraction alone he appears worse; judged on the triglycerides alone he appears transformed; both readings are wrong. The decision is to keep the lifestyle gains, stop treating the calculated value as a comparator at all, and turn attention to the drivers terrain actually identified.

The same method, different territory

Nothing above depends on cardiology. Here is the identical sequence in two other domains.

EXAMPLE 4 · THYROID

A thyroid result that looks like a diagnosis

A woman in her forties presents with fatigue and palpitations. Her thyroid-stimulating hormone comes back suppressed and her free thyroid hormones are elevated — a picture consistent with an overactive thyroid. Treatment is offered at the first appointment.

 Walking the five questions
Q1Free versus total fractions confirmed — the free hormones were measured, which is the right question. Units consistent with the reference range quoted.
Q2Measured by immunoassay rather than calculated. Which matters enormously here, for reasons that emerge at question 3.
Q3She takes a high-dose hair and nail supplement containing biotin, and took it the morning of the draw. Biotin interferes with many streptavidin-based immunoassays, characteristically producing a falsely low stimulating hormone alongside falsely high free hormones — precisely the pattern reported. The FDA issued a safety communication on this in 2017.
Q4Terrain review is still worth doing: her fatigue is real, her sleep is poor, and a period of significant stress preceded the symptoms. Those findings do not disappear if the thyroid result is an artefact.
Q5Rather than an uptake scan, the cheapest next step is a repeat after holding biotin for several days — which resolves the question directly.
◆ THE DECISION
The repeat off biotin returns entirely normal thyroid values. No thyroid disease, no treatment, and a diagnosis avoided that would have followed her for years. The fatigue and palpitations remain real and still require explanation — the terrain question does not go away simply because one test turned out to be an artefact. Question 3 resolved this one, and question 3 costs nothing.
EXAMPLE 5 · GUT

A gut panel with many abnormalities and no obvious target

A man in his fifties with bloating and irregular bowel habit runs a comprehensive stool panel. Roughly a dozen markers are flagged — several organisms outside the reference range, a raised inflammatory marker, low levels of a protective bacterial group. He is given a list of nine supplements.

 Walking the five questions
Q1Reference ranges on panels of this kind are frequently derived from the laboratory’s own population rather than from outcome data, and differ substantially between vendors. A flag means “unusual in this laboratory’s sample”, not necessarily “harmful”.
Q2Several values are derived quantities rather than direct counts. Their precision is lower than the decimal places imply.
Q3He had taken a course of antibiotics six weeks earlier, which substantially perturbs these measurements for months. He had also been travelling.
Q4Terrain review finds the driver almost immediately: profoundly disordered sleep for two years, meals eaten late and at irregular times, and very little movement. The gut findings are plausibly downstream of all three.
Q5The relevant trajectory question is not another panel — it is whether his symptoms track with anything. A two-week symptom and food diary identifies a clear relationship with meal timing.
◆ THE DECISION
The decision is not nine supplements. It is to treat the panel as descriptive rather than prescriptive given the recent antibiotics, address meal timing and sleep first, and re-evaluate in eight weeks with a defined readout — symptom score, not another panel. A dozen abnormal markers is not a dozen problems. It is frequently one upstream problem and a measurement taken at a moment when nothing would have looked normal.

What the five examples have in common

In every one, the decisive move was made before anyone chose a treatment — and in three of the five, the decisive move cost nothing at all.

  • Two were resolved by asking what happened in the 48 hours before the draw.
  • One was resolved by noticing that a number had been calculated rather than measured.
  • One was resolved by asking whether a single upstream cause could explain several findings.
  • One was resolved by obtaining direct evidence instead of arguing about proxies.

None of them required knowing more medicine. They required asking the five questions in order, and being willing to hear that the answer to one of them was “nobody checked”.

Aha Moments

Nine questions with real citations. Each carries its honest limitation inside the moment itself — volunteering the caveat before anyone asks is what converts a surprising claim into a credible one.

They are ordered roughly by how early in a conversation they land well.

Aha Moment 1 · Conventional medicine blind spot
Your thyroid test says your thyroid is overactive. What if it is measuring your hair supplement?
High-dose biotin — the ingredient in most hair, skin and nail products — interferes with many streptavidin-based immunoassays. The characteristic pattern is a falsely low thyroid-stimulating hormone alongside falsely elevated free T4 and T3: a picture that reads as overt hyperthyroidism in someone with an entirely normal thyroid. The same interference affects troponin and several other assays. The FDA issued a formal safety communication about it in 2017.
  • Honest limitation: Not every assay is affected — platforms differ, and some laboratories have moved to biotin-resistant methods. The point is not that thyroid results are unreliable; it is that this specific, common, invisible confounder is almost never asked about. Holding biotin for several days and repeating resolves it.
  • Citation: FDA Safety Communication, November 2017; AACC/ADLM guidance document on biotin interference in laboratory tests.
  • Delivery tip: Ask the room how many take a hair, skin or nail supplement. Count the hands, pause, then answer.
  • Best placed: Opening hook, or immediately before Question 3.
Aha Moment 2 · Conventional medicine blind spot
What if the most useful number on your lab report is one the laboratory never printed?
Non-HDL cholesterol is total cholesterol minus HDL — two numbers already on every standard panel. It captures every atherogenic particle rather than one subset, costs nothing, requires no new test, and is computable from any lipid panel you have ever had. In a meta-analysis of individual data from 62,154 people across 8 statin trials, it was more strongly associated with future major cardiovascular events than either the calculated LDL fraction or the directly measured particle protein.
  • Honest limitation: The better measurement is still the direct particle count; non-HDL is the free approximation you already own. And that analysis was conducted in people already taking statins, which is not everybody.
  • Citation: Boekholdt et al., JAMA, 2012;307(12):1302–1309.
  • Delivery tip: Ask people to find any old lipid panel and do the subtraction live. The arithmetic takes ten seconds and the number is new to almost everyone.
  • Best placed: After Question 2, or as a live activity.
Aha Moment 3 · Conventional medicine blind spot
What if the kidney disease in your chart was created by your workout?
Creatinine is a breakdown product of muscle. It is used as a kidney marker because the kidney clears it — but it is produced in proportion to muscle mass and turnover. Heavy training, high muscle mass, creatine supplementation, sauna use and simple dehydration all raise it, lowering the calculated filtration rate, with no change in actual kidney function. A systematic review of creatine supplementation found a small but statistically significant rise in serum creatinine with no significant change in measured filtration.
  • Honest limitation: An elevated creatinine is never safe to ignore. Cystatin C, which is largely independent of muscle mass, is the cross-check — and it has its own confounders including corticosteroids, thyroid dysfunction and inflammation. The lesson is to repeat under controlled conditions before accepting the label, not to dismiss the finding.
  • Citation: Samra and Abcar, The Permanente Journal, 2012;16(2):51–52; systematic review and meta-analysis of creatine supplementation and kidney function, BMC Nephrology, 2025.
  • Delivery tip: Works especially well with anyone who lifts. Ask who takes creatine before answering.
  • Best placed: Question 3, or the Decision Tree.
Aha Moment 4 · Timeline / causality
What if a number going up was the proof that the treatment worked?
Several widely quoted values are calculated rather than measured, and calculated values have conditions under which the arithmetic simply fails. The most common lipid estimate was published in 1972 with its limitation stated in the original paper — it is not valid above a triglyceride of 400 mg/dL. When triglycerides fall from above that threshold, two things happen at once: cholesterol genuinely redistributes between particle classes, and the equation becomes valid for the first time. The result can rise sharply while the actual atherogenic burden falls.
  • Honest limitation: This describes one specific circumstance — very high triglycerides — not a general rule that rising cholesterol is good news. Below the validity threshold, a rise means what you think it means.
  • Citation: Friedewald, Levy and Fredrickson, Clinical Chemistry, 1972;18(6):499–502; Martin et al., JAMA, 2013;310(19):2061–2068.
  • Delivery tip: Show the before and after, ask for a verdict, then reveal that one of the two numbers was never a measurement.
  • Best placed: Question 2, or Worked Example 3.
Aha Moment 5 · Systems thinking
Two results, 96 and 48. What if they are the same number?
Where one laboratory reports a particle in mass units and another reports it in molar units, the two are not interconvertible, because the relationship depends on how large that individual’s particles happen to be. Published conversion factors span roughly 1.85 to 2.85 depending on the protein isoform. A result of 48 in mass units therefore sits somewhere between about 89 and 137 in molar units — a range wide enough to straddle most decision thresholds. And the error has a direction: the smaller, more atherogenic isoforms carry the higher factor, so mass units systematically under-report risk in exactly the people who have the most of it.
  • Honest limitation: The mass result is not wrong. It simply cannot be compared to a molar result, or to a molar threshold, without knowing the isoform — which is why the field is moving to molar reporting. The fix is to re-order in the preferred units, not to convert the number you have. (Verify exact authorship before citing in print.)
  • Citation: Journal of Clinical Lipidology, 2018 — relationship of molar concentration and mass by apolipoprotein isoform size.
  • Delivery tip: Put both numbers on screen and ask which person is at higher risk. Let the room commit before answering.
  • Best placed: Question 1.
Aha Moment 6 · Brain-body connection
What if the memory test that reads like early dementia is measuring how you slept?
Cognitive testing reports domain scores, and the shape across domains carries more information than any single line. A pattern of slowed processing and impaired consolidation alongside intact attention, executive function and cognitive flexibility looks more like a brain being acted on by something ongoing than like a degenerative process, which does not typically spare higher-order function while gutting speed. In someone with chronic fragmented sleep and heavy snoring, that reorders the investigation entirely — the next test is a sleep study, not a memory clinic.
  • Honest limitation: This rules nothing out, and in someone with genetic risk and a family history it would be dishonest to suggest otherwise. The meta-review literature is explicit that it remains difficult to say which cognitive domains sleep-disordered breathing affects. Several medications also slow processing speed. This sets the order of investigation, not the diagnosis — and repeat testing after treating the sleep is what actually answers it.
  • Citation: Bucks, Olaithe and Eastwood, Respirology, 2013;18(1):61–70.
  • Delivery tip: Show the low score alone first. Let the room sit with it. Then show the whole profile.
  • Best placed: Question 4, or Worked Example 2.
Aha Moment 7 · Systems thinking
Split one DNA sample in two and test both. How far apart can the answers be?
Up to nine years, for six prominent epigenetic clocks. At the level of individual methylation sites reproducibility is poor — across 350 blood samples run twice, probe reliability coefficients skewed hard toward zero, with a mean of 0.21 and a median of 0.09. Aggregating hundreds of sites into a score recovers much of that but not all of it. Retrained using principal-component methods, most replicates came within about 1.5 years. Same DNA, same instant, same laboratory.
  • Honest limitation: This is an argument for reading such results carefully, not for discarding them. The underlying idea — that methylation reflects sustained exposure rather than one morning — is sound and genuinely useful. The practical rule is to trust the direction and the large outliers, and to ignore small differences: a marker at the 53rd percentile and one at the 58th are not telling you different things.
  • Citation: Sugden et al., Patterns, 2020; Higgins-Chen et al., Nature Aging, 2022;2(7):644–661.
  • Delivery tip: Ask for a guess at the spread before giving the number. Guesses cluster around a few months.
  • Best placed: Any discussion of advanced or consumer testing.
Aha Moment 8 · Conventional medicine blind spot
What if the reason a medication gave you side effects was knowable before you took it?
For one widely prescribed drug class, three genes carry actionable evidence, formalised in a 2022 consensus guideline. One encodes the transporter that moves the drug out of the bloodstream and into the liver where it works — a reduced-function variant means more drug remains in systemic circulation, raising exposure in muscle and with it the risk of muscular side effects. Two further genes affect specific individual drugs within the same class. A flagged variant does not mean the treatment goal changes. It means the choice of agent and the starting dose should.
  • Honest limitation: Pharmacogenomic testing is not routine, is not always covered, and its clinical utility varies enormously by drug class — this is not an argument that everyone should be genotyped for everything. It is an argument that where actionable guidance exists, knowing beforehand beats discovering afterwards.
  • Citation: Cooper-DeHoff et al., Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics, 2022;111(5) — CPIC guideline for SLCO1B1, ABCG2 and CYP2C9.
  • Delivery tip: Ask who has stopped a medication because of side effects without ever finding out why.
  • Best placed: Decision Tree, Rule 3 — or any medication conversation.
Aha Moment 9 · Timeline / causality
A gene variant is called the strongest risk factor for a major disease. Why has evolution not removed it?
Because in a different environment it may not have been a liability. In a study of 372 forager-horticulturalists in the Bolivian Amazon — a population in which most adults carry intestinal parasite infection at any time — non-carriers of the variant showed worse cognitive performance as parasite burden rose, while carriers maintained performance even at high burden. The opposite of what the same variant reliably predicts in industrialised populations. This is the antagonistic pleiotropy hypothesis: advantages expressed early, costs arriving decades later.
  • Honest limitation: This is not a reassurance about carrying the variant. In a population like ours the disease risk is real, large and repeatedly replicated. The early-life advantage is genuinely contested — at least one meta-analysis across seven cognitive domains in young adults found no supporting effect. The defensible claim is that the variant is context-dependent, not benign.
  • Citation: Trumble et al., The FASEB Journal, 2017;31(4):1508–1515.
  • Delivery tip: Best used with someone who has just received a frightening genetic result. Deliver it slowly.
  • Best placed: Closing, or any genetics conversation.

What makes these work

Each one exposes an assumption that has never been examined, and each resolves into something actionable rather than simply unsettling. That distinction matters — a surprising claim that leaves someone frightened and without a next step has done harm, not good.

Notice also that four of the nine turn on nothing more than the first three questions — units, measured-or-calculated, and the 48 hours before the draw. The most striking moments in clinical reasoning are rarely about knowing more medicine. They are about checking whether the number was real before building anything on it.

◆ The RoVive Clinical Framework
These four stages — Roots, Reveal, Refine, Rise — are the RoVive diagnostic and therapeutic sequence. Here they are applied to the act of deciding itself.
ROOTS — what any good decision requires before anything else

Four things have to be in place before a decision is worth making, and none of them is a test.

A stated question. “Should I take this?” is not answerable. “Is this marker elevated because of something I can change, and is it currently causing harm?” is.

A known baseline. If you do not know where you started, you cannot know whether anything moved. This applies to symptoms as much as to markers, and symptom baselines are almost never recorded.

An honest inventory. Everything currently being taken, including things bought without a prescription, and everything currently being done. Decisions made against an incomplete list are made against fiction.

A named goal. What is actually being optimised for — which may not be what a guideline assumes. Two people with identical results can reasonably choose differently, and the difference usually lives here.

REVEAL — what to measure, in what order

Testing has a sequence, and the sequence is what stops it becoming a shopping trip.

Layer 1 — Foundational. Standard panels from a major laboratory ✓ RWC Lab Store. Inexpensive, well standardised, well understood, and sufficient to answer a surprising proportion of questions. Start here every time.

Layer 2 — Functional. Hormone, gut, toxin and nutrient panels ✓ RWC Lab Store. Appropriate when a foundational result raised a specific question that a foundational test cannot answer.

Layer 3 — Advanced and metabolomic. Comprehensive metabolomics, methylation-based measures, genetic panels ✓ RWC Lab Store. Genuinely informative, frequently over-ordered, and most useful when there is a specific hypothesis to test rather than a general wish for more data.

◆ THE SEQUENCING RULE
Foundational first. Act on it for 8–12 weeks. Escalate only where a real question remains unanswered. A panel ordered because the uncertainty is uncomfortable will produce findings, and those findings will produce more testing, and none of it will have been driven by a question.

Physical capacity belongs in this layer and is routinely forgotten. Grip strength and gait speed are validated predictors of all-cause mortality, cost nothing, and can be measured in a corridor. Body composition — particularly visceral fat and appendicular lean mass rather than a single body fat percentage — answers questions no blood marker can, and is essential for anyone whose weight is changing for any reason.

REFINE — the levers, in order of leverage

Once the number is established and the driver identified, interventions are ordered by leverage rather than by novelty.

1. The upstream driver, if one was found. Treating it resolves several downstream findings at once.

2. The terrain inputs — the Great 8 Pillars, starting with whichever is most obviously deficient. These are usually free, usually slower, and usually more durable.

3. Targeted correction of anything genuinely deficient, with a defined endpoint and a plan to stop when it is reached.

4. Agents that modify the marker directly — pharmaceutical or nutraceutical — once the layers above have been addressed or deliberately ruled out with reasons.

The best medicine is the one you actually believe will work. Different people respond to different modalities — not because of placebo, but because belief, expectation, and neurological state are themselves physiological inputs. Choose what resonates.

RISE — what success actually looks like

Success in a decision protocol is not a set of numbers. It is a change in how decisions get made.

In the results: the markers that were pre-specified as the readout have moved, in the direction and on the timescale that was named in advance.

In the experience: you can say what each item on your plan is for, what it is doing, and what would make you stop it. Nothing is on the list because somebody once suggested it.

In your capacity: you walk into an appointment with a written question rather than a worry, and you can tell the difference between a clinician who has reasoned about your case and one who has pattern-matched it.

And the quiet one: the number of things you are taking has gone down at least once, deliberately, because a review found something that was no longer earning its place.

◆ THE EMOTIONAL ROOT
Physical symptoms are real. And they often have an emotional architecture beneath them — not as weakness, but as the body’s intelligence. Unresolved emotional patterns, stored stress, and unconscious adaptations can maintain the very physiology we’re trying to change. This isn’t about blame. It’s about completeness.

The emotional pattern in decision-making

This protocol has an emotional root that is different from a condition protocol’s, and naming it changes how the whole document reads.

Most over-testing and most over-treating are driven by fear, not by evidence. An abnormal result produces genuine anxiety, and doing something — anything — relieves it. That relief is real, it arrives immediately, and it is almost entirely independent of whether the action helped. So the action gets repeated, and the list grows, and each addition buys a little more relief and a little more complexity.

The same fear runs in the opposite direction. Declining an intervention can be its own form of avoidance — the discomfort of committing to something is relieved by not committing, and that relief also feels like judgement.

The sentence that resolves it: uncertainty is not the same as danger, and action is not the same as care. Sitting with a not-yet-answered question, having named what would answer it and by when, is one of the most clinically sophisticated things a person can do — and it feels, in the moment, like doing nothing.

The best approach is the one you believe will work and will actually do. Different people respond to entirely different modalities — that is not noise, it is biology.

◆ WHEN THE DRIVER IS HEALTH ANXIETY

More testing reliably makes it worse, because every broad panel generates incidental findings and each one generates a new worry. The intervention is usually fewer, better-chosen tests with pre-specified readouts — and often direct support for the anxiety itself, which is a legitimate clinical target rather than a character flaw.

◆ WHEN THE DRIVER IS GRIEF OR FAMILY HISTORY

Watching a parent or sibling decline shapes every subsequent decision, usually invisibly. Naming it out loud — “I think I am making this decision about my father” — frequently changes it. Family history is a reason to look carefully. It is not a verdict, and it is not an inheritance you have already received.

◆ WHEN THE DRIVER IS CONTROL

A long regimen can be a way of managing helplessness rather than a way of managing physiology. This is worth recognising with compassion rather than judgement — and it usually responds better to one thing done well and tracked honestly than to being told to stop.

◆ WHEN THE DRIVER IS MISTRUST

Often earned, through a real experience of being dismissed. The risk is that it hardens into refusing everything conventional on principle, which is the same error as accepting everything on principle. Scepticism applied evenly is discernment; scepticism applied in one direction only is a position.

◆ WHERE TO START
NEW TO THIS
Take the most recent result that worries you and answer Question 3 — what was going on in the 48 hours before that draw? It costs nothing, it takes two minutes, and it resolves a surprising number of abnormal results on its own.
ALREADY DOING THE BASICS
Take everything currently on your plan and give each item a review date and a named trigger for stopping. Anything you cannot write a trigger for is a candidate for the next conversation.

The Sentences

“A result is not an observation. It is the output of a procedure — and the procedure can fail while nothing is wrong with you.”

“An abnormal result is never safe to ignore, and rarely safe to act on the first time you see it.”

“If you cannot name what would change your mind, you have not made a decision. You have taken a position.”

“Four findings do not necessarily mean four problems.”

“A modest intervention that happens beats an excellent one that does not.”
